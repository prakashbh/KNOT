The pilot study was the second stage in a research journey that began with initial classroom exploration and feedback in 2022–23. It involved a different student batch from both the initial exploration and the later official study. In the pilot, I tested a wider range of learning activities and gathered student feedback, assessment records, case studies, lab work, interviews, and project evidence. The survey questions and items were still unclear, and the analyses were exploratory: at this point, I was learning what could be examined and what needed to be refined. This work helped the first KNOT framework version emerge, and showed how the later official study needed clearer questions and measures.

Initial pilot research question

How can Problem-Based Learning and Computational Thinking be used together to support student learning?

This broad question guided an exploratory pilot before knowledge construction became the defined focus of the later research question. It offered an early view of learning processes that merited closer study: interpreting a problem, decomposing it, recognising patterns, abstracting useful principles, designing an algorithm, reflecting on a decision, and applying learning in a new context. These observations helped shape the more focused research question developed after the pilot.

6.1 Pilot design

A pilot as a space to observe and refine

The assessments in this semester did two things at once. They gave students different ways to work with problems and algorithms, and they helped me see what kinds of learning each activity made visible. The portfolio encouraged curiosity without marks. Case studies and reflections showed how students interpreted authentic situations. Lab exercises revealed how they approached implementation and efficiency. The written examination showed how they reasoned individually. Project work brought several ideas together in a problem context chosen by each student.

The pilot therefore treated assessment as a set of windows into learning rather than as a single measure of success. Across these activities, the questions were: how do students make sense of a problem, what helps them organise their thinking, how do they respond to guidance, and when do they begin to connect ideas across contexts?

6.2 Data sources

What the pilot evidence can tell us

The evidence comes from several sources collected during a Design and Analysis of Algorithms course. The student-reported understanding survey and the portfolio feedback survey each had 100 respondents. The pre- and post-course interviews involved the same 22 students. The paired case-study and lab outputs report 129 observations, and the project dataset contains 129 student records. In the written examination, students answered three of four questions; therefore, the number of responses differs by item.

How to read the pilot counts. Each number belongs to a particular pilot instrument or assessment record. Some students did not continue through the course, some did not complete a survey, and the examination allowed students to answer three of four questions. The counts are retained as reported for this exploratory stage and are kept separate from the official study evidence.

The sources offer complementary views of the pilot: surveys capture students’ perceptions, examination and project scores record performance on particular tasks, and interviews describe students’ accounts of learning. Read together, they helped identify patterns and questions that informed the next research stage.

Before the pilot surveys, all participants were informed that the data collected would be used for research purposes. A consent statement on the standard survey form recorded their consent before they proceeded.

The course used a mixture of traditional explanation, storytelling, activity-based teaching, and PBL. The topic and assessment patterns described in this chapter helped identify promising design choices and questions for the next stage of the research.

6.3 Quantitative evidence

Patterns across activities and assessments

The quantitative material shows how students rated learning activities, how they performed across different assessment tasks, and how their project choices reflected what they were prepared to use. The sections below keep those forms of evidence separate before bringing them together.

Each analysis below has a specific exploratory purpose. Descriptive statistics summarise responses and scores; Shapiro–Wilk tests check distribution shape; paired tests examine score differences for the same students; correlations examine whether two measures vary together; and the project-indicator analyses explore whether three project measures show a common pattern. These tests describe this pilot dataset. They do not establish that a teaching mode caused a result or validate the final KNOT framework.

6.3.1 Portfolio and course delivery

Curiosity first, then formal concepts

The portfolio brought together sixteen case studies drawn from nature, history, art, and everyday systems. Students had two weeks to explore them and prepare short reports, usually in teams of four. The work was not graded. Its purpose was to give students room to notice patterns, make comparisons, and connect algorithmic ideas with familiar situations before focusing on formal techniques.

The portfolio feedback survey had 100 responses. Forty-five students (45%) rated the activity highly effective, and 40 (40%) rated it effective. Seven (7%) selected neutral, one (1%) selected ineffective, and seven (7%) selected highly ineffective. The 85% positive response suggests that students valued the exploratory format. Because the activity was ungraded and the feedback was self-reported, it indicates perceived usefulness rather than measured learning gain or the quality of each student portfolio.

Why compare delivery modes? The topic plan was compared with the understanding survey to see whether students’ reported clarity varied across topics taught through different modes. Across the 35 topics that could be matched unambiguously, the average proportion reporting “completely understood” was 70.5% for PBL topics, 73.5% for storytelling-supported topics, 90.3% for activity-based topics, and 62.9% for traditional topics. “Efficiency Analysis” is not included because the course plan contains two similarly named entries taught in different ways.

This is a descriptive comparison, not a statistical test of teaching effectiveness: the topics differed in complexity, mode was not assigned randomly, and the activity-based group contained only three topics. Topic-level values were more informative for planning support. For example, 90% reported complete understanding of Dijkstra’s algorithm, compared with 20% for Sparse Table. These patterns prompted closer attention to task, scaffolding, and topic complexity alongside teaching mode; they do not show that one mode caused better understanding.

Why summarise and compare the lab dimensions? Descriptive statistics show the typical score and spread for each dimension. Shapiro–Wilk checks found that each score distribution departed from normality (p < .001). The pilot also reports a paired t-test because the same students have both scores; this tests whether the mean scores differ, not whether students improved from a baseline. The reported test was t(128) = −15.7, p < .001. The available normality output checks each score separately, not the within-student difference scores required to assess the paired t-test assumption, so this test is retained as an exploratory result and interpreted cautiously.

The correlation analysis asks whether the two dimensions varied together across students. Spearman’s rank correlation is foregrounded because it does not require normally distributed scores; Pearson’s r is retained as a familiar linear-association summary. Both estimates are positive and statistically significant, indicating that students with higher problem-solving scores also tended to report higher self-directed-learning scores in this pilot. This association does not show that one dimension caused the other.

6.4 Qualitative evidence

How students described their learning

Interviews with the same 22 students before and after the course provide a view of how students described their understanding. The material is organised by question and summarised descriptively to identify recurring contrasts in students’ accounts. These summaries served as exploratory input that helped refine the research focus; the formal qualitative findings from the official study are presented separately.

Question 1

What are data structures and algorithms?

Before: Students often described storage, organisation, operations, and programming techniques. Few explained why a structure or algorithm mattered or how it shaped efficiency.

After: Responses more often described DSA as a way to reason about problems, compare trade-offs, and make design choices. Students named decomposition, abstraction, optimisation, and practical use.

The idea moved from what it contains toward how it helps us think and choose.

Question 2

How does course material connect to real situations?

Before: Students described few connections beyond assignments and exam practice, and were unsure how algorithms appeared in real systems.

After: Case studies and project work helped students explain why a technique might be useful in a transport, web, water, or other applied context.

Meaningful problem contexts helped connect classroom concepts with use beyond the classroom.

Question 3

What is problem solving, and how do you do it?

Before: Students often spoke of trying different methods or finding an answer. Few mentioned analysing constraints, identifying patterns, or breaking a task into parts.

After: Students more often described understanding the problem, identifying knowns and unknowns, decomposing it, comparing options, and reflecting on a solution.

Problem solving was described more as a deliberate and revisable process than as trial and error.

Question 4

How do you learn?

Before: Students relied mainly on videos, reading, and listening, often with an exam focus.

After: Students described learning through practice, implementation, discussion, repeated problem solving, and reflection, alongside the same learning resources.

The account of learning shifted toward doing, testing, and revisiting ideas.

Across the four questions, the interviews suggest changes in students’ language and in how they connected concepts to problems. The accounts describe a movement toward more structured reasoning and active learning. Together, the themes helped shape the final study’s focus on knowledge construction and student learning transitions.

6.5 Discussion

What the patterns suggest together

Learning depended on how the topic met the student

The strongest survey ratings did not belong to one teaching mode in every case. Activity-based sorting topics drew high reports of complete understanding. Storytelling supported topics such as 2–3 Trees and AVL Trees. PBL appeared promising for graphs, tree operations, recursion, and shortest-path problems. Yet some difficult PBL topics, including Sparse Table and Heap, remained challenging. The pilot points toward a design that chooses its support to suit the thinking a topic requires.

Context made algorithms easier to use and discuss

Portfolio, case-study, and project evidence points in the same direction: students valued opportunities to see algorithms in a wider setting. The project-selection counts also suggest that students more often applied several PBL-taught techniques. The pattern connects course concepts with students’ project selections and highlights the role of context, topic familiarity, and course emphasis.

Computational thinking became more visible in student reasoning

The interview summaries, written-exam mapping, lab scores, and project indicators all draw attention to decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, and algorithm design. The project data show that these processes could be observed in students’ decisions and work products. These processes became central dimensions in the final research design, where decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, and algorithm design were considered across student work and reports.

Learning transitions need deliberate support

Students’ accounts move from uncertainty or intuition toward more organised explanations, but the movement is not automatic. A learner may understand a familiar algorithm and still struggle to transfer it to a new problem. Storytelling, visual models, case studies, guided practice, and reflection can provide different kinds of support as students move between prior ideas and more principled understanding.

Liminality offers a useful lens for interpreting these transitions: students may pass through an in-between period when an old explanation no longer works and a new one is still forming. These experiences led to liminality being used as an interpretive lens in the final study, alongside student accounts and assessment evidence.

6.6 Research question development

From an exploratory question to a focused study

The pilot began with a broad question about bringing PBL and CT together to support student learning. It guided an exploration of how students experienced PBL and CT before the research question and framework were refined.

Initial pilot research question

How can Problem-Based Learning and Computational Thinking be used together to support student learning?

As the pilot evidence accumulated, attention turned to how students made sense of problems, organised their reasoning, worked through uncertainty, and applied ideas. This led to a more focused research question centred on knowledge construction, followed by sub-questions about design elements, learning evidence, student experience, and liminality. The refined question and its sub-questions were developed after the pilot and are set out in Chapter 8.

6.7 Framework design

A first PBL–CT framework from the pilot

The framework emerging from this pilot treats learning as movement through authentic problem situations, computational thinking, uncertainty, and knowledge construction. PBL provides the setting in which students meet a problem that matters. CT offers ways to make that problem manageable: decompose it, recognise patterns, abstract essential ideas, and design an algorithm. Reflection and scaffolding help students examine their decisions as their understanding changes.

01Authentic problemA situation with context, ambiguity, and more than one possible route
→
02Inquiry and uncertaintyStudents surface what they know and what they need to understand
→
03Computational thinkingDecomposition · patterns · abstraction · algorithm design

04Knowledge constructionExplain, test, revise, connect, and apply ideas in a new context

Case studies, storytelling, visual models, guided activities, lab work, and reflective prompts can support different points in this movement. They offer structure without removing the need for students to reason through the problem themselves. When learners explain why a solution works, revise it after feedback, and carry the underlying idea into a different situation, their understanding becomes more coherent and transferable.

This first design proposition guided the next stage of the research: examining how the elements work together, which supports are useful at different moments, and how students’ knowledge construction can be understood through multiple forms of evidence.

6.8 From pilot to research design

What the pilot contributed and what remained exploratory

The pilot is part of the research record, and its evidence remains reported in this chapter. Some findings informed the official research design; others remain useful for understanding how the course developed, but were not used as primary evidence when interpreting the final study.

Informed the official study

Retain meaningful problems and visible reasoning

The pilot supported continuing with an integrated PBL–CT course in Design and Analysis of Algorithms. The official study examined knowledge construction alongside self-directed learning, problem solving, transfer of knowledge, and students’ experiences of uncertainty and transition.

Authentic problem cases, design intuitions, classroom activities, laboratory work, projects, and reflective case studies remained relevant parts of the course. The official study examined these through the survey, concept-understanding evidence, laboratory and case-study work, project evaluations, and students’ reflections on learning transitions.

Refined for the official study

Clarify questions, constructs, and measures

The pilot’s surveys, task scores, project indicators, and interview themes helped identify what to ask and observe. The official study used measures aligned with the defined constructs, specified scoring procedures, and internal-consistency estimates to interpret its survey domains cautiously.

Qualitative reflections and open responses were analysed through the framework method. Liminality guided interpretation of students’ reported transitions.

Remain exploratory pilot analyses

Keep in the record, not as final-study claims

Topic averages by teaching mode, written-exam item results, and the pilot’s paired case-study comparisons remain part of the pilot record. They informed the official study, but are not treated as causal evidence about the final framework.

The pilot case-study reliability estimates and project-indicator analyses remain exploratory pilot findings. Project size and algorithm-count correlations provide descriptive context. The official study used its defined domains and evidence sources to examine knowledge construction.
