2.1 Introduction

Introduction

This chapter examines the foundations and developments of Problem-Based Learning (PBL) and Computational Thinking (CT) through the central lens of knowledge construction. The aim is to understand how these two pedagogical approaches have evolved and how their integration can enhance problem solving, self-directed learning, and transfer of knowledge among computer science and engineering students. The review begins by tracing the origins of PBL in the medical domain and situating it within broader theoretical traditions. It then explores models, frameworks, and learning theories that support PBL, alongside a chronological analysis of its evolution over the past decades. Subsequently, the discussion turns to computational thinking, its role in shaping problem-solving practices, and the emerging intersections of CT and PBL across domains. Mapping existing contributions, debates, and limitations, the chapter identifies gaps that motivate the design of a new framework tailored to knowledge construction in CSE education. The overall flow of this chapter is presented in Figure 2.1 below.

The review begins by framing knowledge construction through problem solving, self-directed learning, and knowledge transfer, which naturally leads to tracing the roots of PBL in medical education. From its historical origins, the discussion progresses to the theoretical foundations like constructivism, social learning, and experiential learning: that explains its pedagogical value. These theories shape the evolution and adaptations of PBL across domains, giving rise to diverse models and instructional frameworks. Examining these models necessitates a critical appraisal of their strengths and limitations, which in turn opens space for considering complementary paradigms. Here, computational thinking (CT) emerges as a significant corresponding, moving from its principles and definitions to its applications in education and problem-solving practices. The intersections of PBL and CT highlight their potential synergies, leading to an identification of research gaps and implications that justify the need for a new integrative framework.

2.2 Foundations of Learning

Foundations of Learning

Learning is a state of transformation that results in observable change, either immediately or over time. It can occur in structured, semi-structured, or unstructured environments. For a new-born child, learning often emerges through unstructured repetition of behaviours, whereas later in life it takes the form of discovering new meanings and abstractions of the known. Across the human life cycle, learning oscillates between necessity and compulsion. No single definition fully captures all its dimensions, whether viewed as acquiring knowledge, becoming a domain expert, or engaging in meaning-making. Although theories of learning have been questioned (Skinner, 1950), experimental studies continue to validate methods and explore their effectiveness. From sensory stimulation theories to andragogy, scholars have investigated principles of learning from diverse perspectives (Dunn, 2002).

During schooling and higher education, the primary objective is to identify and strengthen skills through varied forms and styles of learning. While formal education enforces structure, learners often engage in a mix of structured and semi-structured patterns. Social contexts also shape learning styles and mechanisms, positioning learning as both an individual activity and a community-oriented goal (Pashler et al., 2008).

The definition of learning further shifts across individual and collective settings. Individual learning contributes to personal growth and, indirectly, to organizations and society. For example, a toddler learning to walk or run eventually acquires higher-order skills such as driving. Necessities are therefore context-dependent and evolve over time. Importantly, conscious living and learning are not synonymous, despite appearing so (Jarvis, 2012). Within social and community contexts, learning objectives vary from necessary to required practices depending on cultural significance. For instance, one learner may be encouraged to recite a morning prayer, while another is expected to follow a methodical, ritualized version of the same (Bereiter, 1990).

Learning is also shaped by systemic structures. Education systems, workplaces, and personal contexts all present problems that require thinking, applying, and adapting cognitive activities deeply influenced by social interactions (Rogoff & Lave, 1984). Formal institutions, therefore, play a crucial role in setting contexts that influence learners beyond the boundaries of time-limited programs. This explains why systems of education remain under constant research, experimentation, and redesign to maximize effectiveness (Weinstein & Underwood, 1985; Hasan et al., 2021). Lifelong learning, central to sustainable development goals (Elfert, 2019) emerges as a key outcome, preparing individuals to adapt to future problem-solving circumstances.

Across history, educational models have reflected these evolving purposes. The Gurukul system in India immersed students in experiential, community-based learning (Kashalkar-Karve & Damodar, 2013). Later, the library-based model emphasized centralized resources, while today, digital and blended learning dominate (Valiathan, 2002; Singh, 2021). Pedagogical models such as brain-based learning, constructivism, and competency-based frameworks continue to inform debates on effective learning strategies (Gülpinar, 2005; Voorhees, 2001).

Despite these variations, the fundamentals of learning consistently revolve around problems (Thorndike, 1932). Formal systems generally emphasize instruction, process, outcomes, and performance (Mayer, 1988), but individual learning trajectories are shaped by cognitive levels, prior knowledge, social-emotional factors, and personal histories. To address this variability, instruments such as the Index of Learning Styles have been developed (Felder & Solomon, 2000).

The limitations of the traditional “bucket theory,” which views students as empty vessels to be filled with knowledge, have long motivated the search for more engaging and meaningful approaches to learning (Wood, 1994). At the center of many of these approaches lies the problem: it can be deliberately structured to guide learning, or learning itself can be organized around it. The discussion thus far highlights how diverse perspectives on learning, spanning individual, social, cultural, and institutional dimensions, eventually converge on the centrality of problems. Whether approached through traditional systems or modern pedagogical frameworks, problems serve as both the trigger and the medium for learning. This recognition has given rise to instructional models that explicitly organize the learning process around problem situations. Among these, PBL has emerged as a particularly influential approach, rooted in established theories yet continually evolving to address contemporary educational needs. Functioning as an instructional strategy grounded in real-world challenges, PBL encourages active learning, critical thinking, collaboration, and adaptability. Drawing from constructivism, situated cognition, cognitive flexibility, social learning, apprenticeship, experiential learning, and inquiry-based theories, it positions the problem as the driving force of meaningful learning.

Learning lies at the core, explained by theories, situated in context, implemented through systems, realized in practice through problems and pedagogy and moderated by factors. Factors are the contextual influences such as learner diversity, resources, and environment that eventually shape how learning theories, systems, and pedagogies translate into practice. 2.3 Problem-Based Learning

Problem Based Learning: Origins and Foundations

PBL emerged as a transformative approach in the late twentieth century, initially pioneered by Barrows in medical education to prepare learners for real-world professional challenges. Its design is deeply rooted in multiple learning theories, including constructivism, situated cognition, cognitive flexibility, social learning, experiential and inquiry-based learning, as well as apprenticeship models, all of which emphasize active, contextual, and collaborative knowledge construction. Building on these foundations, several universities developed early PBL models most notably McMaster, Maastricht, and Aalborg, each contributing distinctive philosophies and practices that shaped PBL’s evolution and set the stage for its broader application across disciplines. The following discussion traces these developments along the lines of history, theoretical foundations and early PBL models.

Historical Roots

PBL can be traced to the 1950s and 1960s, with its origins in medical education (Barrows, 1996). Neurology experts observed that traditional methods hindered the development of clinical reasoning, underlining the need for approaches centred on problem-solving, an essential competency in healthcare (Barrows & Bennett, 1972). Enhancing teaching effectiveness required medical instructors to understand clinical reasoning processes (Barrows & Feltovich, 1987). One early innovation was the P4: Portable Patient Problem Pack (Barrows & Tamblyn, 1977), developed to address the inadequacy of written and oral exams in evaluating skills such as history taking, diagnosis, and management (Barrows & Abrahamson, 1964). These concerns collectively laid the foundation for PBL as a method designed to stand-in reasoning and problem-solving abilities (Barrows, 1984).

McMaster University played a pivotal role in formalizing PBL by embedding self-directed, problem-based, and small-group tutorial learning into its curriculum. Its model emphasized diagnostic reasoning, judicious use of resources, integrated learning, and structured educational planning (Neufeld & Barrows, 1974). Hallmark characteristics included student-centred learning, small-group collaboration, teacher facilitation, and problems serving as the central stimulus for knowledge acquisition (Barrows & Tamblyn, 1980). The approach was designed to achieve objectives such as structuring domain-specific knowledge, cultivating reasoning processes, developing self-directed learning skills, and enhancing motivation (Barrows, 1985). Empirical studies confirmed these benefits, showing increased motivation, problem-solving, and self-study skills among students (Barrows & Tamblyn, 1976; Berner et al., 1974).

To strengthen inquiry as a core skill in clinical practice, McMaster employed strategies such as simulated patients and human simulations, emphasizing problems as vehicles for reasoning and decision-making (Barrows, 1987; 1990). Tutors did not lecture, prescribe resources, or validate solutions but instead facilitated learning as peers, modeling effective preparation strategies (Barrows, 1988). Guidelines later emphasized self-directed learning as critical for building knowledge required for patient care and long-term professional success (Barrows, 1994). To make a note, PBL was not conceived as a discrete instructional technique but as a way of structuring the entire curriculum, enabling students to engage in clinical reasoning and problem-solving from the outset of their education (Barrows & Kelson, 1996).

Over time, PBL evolved into a constructivist environment that integrates collaborative problem-solving, reflection, and collective knowledge building (Hmelo-Silver & Barrows, 2008). Specific facilitation strategies including metacognitive questioning, re-voicing, hypothesis evaluation, and mapping were developed to help students construct causal explanations, reason effectively, and sustain self-directed learning (Hmelo-Silver & Barrows, 2006). Technological innovations, such as computer-supported collaborative learning, further extended its application (Koschmann et al., 1996).

PBL emerged as a synthesis of validated principles rather than a single prescriptive method. Rooted in the philosophy of beginning with a problem and learning through discovery, it was initially operationalized through “problem boxes” or simulated patient cases (Barrows & Mitchell, 1975). A taxonomy proposed by Barrows allows instructors to select and combine design variables, as outlined in Table 2.1, which summarizes the types, their characteristics, associated skills, and inherent limitations.

Theoretical Foundations of PBL

Learning theories provide the foundation for many of the principles that PBL builds on. Each theory contributes certain elements like active learning, reflection, collaboration, or authentic problem-solving that align closely with the core features of PBL. These common elements help explain why PBL works across different contexts and how it supports deeper understanding and skill development. The Table 2.2 below highlights how major learning theories connect with PBL, showing their shared elements and the kind of learning outcomes they encourage. These theories converge on the idea that learning is most effective when learners actively engage with problems, construct meaning from them, and transfer that understanding to new situations.

Each of these theoretical foundations is discussed in the following subsections. They present how different perspectives on learning converge to strengthen and justify the use of PBL as an effective approach.

PBL borrows ideas from many learning theories, which together shape its philosophy and practice. From constructivism, it takes the view that learners build their own understanding rather than just absorb facts. Cognitive Flexibility Theory influences the way PBL uses open-ended, messy problems to push students to see issues from multiple angles. The focus on real-world contexts in PBL comes directly from situated cognition, while the collaborative, discussion-driven process is rooted in social learning theory. The mentor-like role of the facilitator in PBL draws on the idea of apprenticeship, where guidance is gradually reduced as learners gain confidence. PBL’s iterative process of action, reflection, and application closely aligns with the principles of experiential learning. The spirit of inquiry-based learning is at the heart of PBL, with students driven by questions and exploration rather than ready-made answers. In this way, PBL is less a stand-alone invention and more a blend of insights drawn from these traditions, reworked into a problem-centred approach.

Constructivism

Learning develops through interaction with the real world, and constructivism provides a framework to explain this process. It views learners as active participants who build knowledge by integrating new experiences with their existing understandings (Moreno et al., 2007). Central concepts include knowledge construction, active engagement, collaboration, contextualized learning, authentic experiences, reflection, metacognition, and assessment as learning (Amineh & Asl, 2015). Social interaction plays a central role, as dialogue, collaboration, and exposure to multiple perspectives deepen understanding (Lowenthal & Muth, 2008). Meaningful learning emerges when individuals connect knowledge to real-life contexts, supported by teachers who guide rather than transmit information.

Prominent thinkers have shaped constructivism across decades. Piaget emphasized cognitive development through assimilation and accommodation (1952), Vygotsky highlighted the social and cultural dimensions of learning (1978), Bruner advanced discovery and inquiry-based approaches (1961), Dewey stressed experience and real-world connections (1938), and Ausubel emphasized the role of meaningful learning structures (1968). Hoover later reinforced the importance of reflection and time in building new knowledge (1996).

In How We Think, Dewey (1910) describes reflective thought as beginning with a felt difficulty and moving through the consideration and examination of possible explanations. This account helps explain how a problem can become an occasion for inquiry and reflection.

Constructivism continues to influence curriculum design, teaching strategies, and assessment practices. Its focus on active engagement, social interaction, and authentic problem-solving supports deeper understanding, critical thinking, and application of knowledge in diverse learning contexts.

The key points of Constructivism are:

  • Learners build understanding by integrating new experiences with prior knowledge, engaging in exploration, questioning, problem-solving, and reflection rather than passively receiving information.
  • Dialogue, collaboration, and authentic real-world contexts enhance learning, allowing students to negotiate meaning, consider diverse perspectives, and apply knowledge to meaningful situations.
  • Teachers act as facilitators providing support and resources, while learners develop metacognitive skills through reflection and continuous assessment to monitor and refine their learning.

Cognitive Flexibility Theory

Cognitive Flexibility Theory highlights the capacity to adapt thinking strategies and shift across cognitive frameworks in response to changing situations. It features the importance of flexibility in problem-solving, learning, and transferring knowledge (Rende, 2000).

Jonassen examines how instructional design can support both well-structured and ill-structured problems, situating Cognitive Flexibility Theory at the center of this discussion. The theory communicates the complexity of problems by presenting information through multiple representations or thematic perspectives. Learners are encouraged to compare and contrast across cases, enabling the construction of more robust knowledge structures. A defining feature of instruction for ill-structured problems lies in the deliberate inclusion of diverse perspectives and interpretations (Jonassen, 1997).

According to this perspective, learners with greater cognitive flexibility demonstrate skills that allow them to approach problems in dynamic ways. They are able to recognize and evaluate multiple perspectives, adjust strategies to suit different contexts, restructure prior knowledge when encountering new information, and transfer their understanding to novel situations (Spiro et al., 2003; Spiro, 1988).

Instructional practices that nurture these abilities often involve approaches such as problem-based learning, case-based learning, inquiry-driven exploration, and simulations that actively engage learners (Boger-Mehall, 1996).

The key points of Cognitive Flexibility Theory are:

  • Cognitive Flexibility Theory highlights the importance of being able to switch strategies, restructure knowledge, and consider multiple perspectives to handle ill-structured, complex problems.
  • Learners with higher cognitive flexibility can transfer learning across contexts, applying knowledge and strategies to new or unfamiliar situations by recognizing patterns and similarities.
  • The theory advocates using methods like PBL, case-based learning, and simulations that expose learners to multiple perspectives, encourage exploration, and promote flexible thinking.

Situated Cognition

Situated Cognition emphasizes the role of environment and context in shaping learning. Knowledge is tied to the situations, activities, and social interactions where it occurs, making learning a socially mediated process rather than a purely internal one (Roth & Jornet, 2013). Core elements include the contextual nature of learning, active participation, authentic tasks, distributed cognition, and community-based learning (Brown et al., 1989).

Context strongly influences how knowledge is acquired, applied, and transferred. Learners construct knowledge through active engagement in meaningful tasks, problem-solving, and reflection (Smith & Semin, 2004). Social interaction further deepens understanding through dialogue, negotiation, and collaboration (Wilson & Myers, 2000). Authentic, real-world tasks enhance transfer of learning, while tools and artefacts distribute cognition beyond the individual. Communities of practice provide apprenticeship, mentorship, and shared practices that support sustained learning (Robbins & Aydede, 2009).

Jean Lave’s work on communities of practice highlights learning as a social and cultural phenomenon. Her idea of “legitimate peripheral participation” describes how newcomers gradually become full participants through authentic involvement (Lave & Wenger, 1991). Situated Cognition highlights the interaction between individuals, environments, and communities. It calls for meaningful, authentic experiences that promote engagement, collaboration, and application of knowledge. Its alignment with Problem-Based Learning lies in shared elements of authentic contexts, social interaction, transfer of knowledge, and assessment, enabling deep understanding and real-world problem-solving.

The key points of Situated Cognition are:

  • Knowledge is shaped by the environment, culture, and social interactions in which it occurs, making learning inseparable from its context.
  • Learners construct knowledge through participation in meaningful, real-world tasks that mirror practical applications.
  • Cognition extends beyond the individual, drawing on tools, artefacts, and collaboration within communities of practice to support deeper understanding.

Social Learning Theory

Social Learning Theory, proposed by Albert Bandura in the 1960s, explains how people acquire behaviours, attitudes, and skills through observation, imitation, and modeling (Bandura & Walters, 1977). It highlights the role of social interaction and environment, showing that individuals learn from direct experience and also by observing others in real life or through symbolic models in media and literature. Core principles of the theory include observational learning, modeling, reinforcement, and self-efficacy (Pratt et al., 2010).

Learners attend to the actions of others, the outcomes of those actions, and imitate behaviours more readily when models are respected, relatable, or rewarded. Reinforcement further shapes learning: positive outcomes increase the likelihood of imitation, while negative consequences reduce it (McLeod, 2011). A central element is self-efficacy, the belief in one’s own ability to perform behaviours successfully, which strongly influences persistence and learning (Bandura, 1969).

The key points of Social Learning Theory are:

  • People acquire behaviours and skills by watching others, whether real-life figures or symbolic models.
  • Positive outcomes increase the chance of imitation, while negative outcomes discourage it.
  • Belief in one’s own capability strongly influences learning and behaviour.

Apprenticeship

Apprenticeship combines workplace learning with formal instruction, creating a structured pathway for developing both practical expertise and theoretical understanding (Dennen & Burner, 2008). Rooted in traditions dating back to ancient guilds, this model established a system where learners advanced under the guidance of skilled masters, a structure that still shapes training today (Steedman et al., 1998).

A central aspect of apprenticeship is on-the-job training. Learners work directly with experienced professionals, observing, practicing, and applying skills in real-world settings while receiving continuous mentorship and feedback (Wolter & Ryan, 2011). This immersive engagement deepens trade-specific knowledge and strengthens practical competence.

Classroom instruction complements workplace practice. Apprentices attend schools or training centres where structured curricula outline progressive skill development, ensuring a balance between applied and formal learning (Ryan, 1998; Hamilton, 2010). These plans, often designed jointly by educators and industry, align training with professional standards and long-term career growth.

The key points of Apprenticeship are:

  • Apprenticeships combine hands-on workplace training with formal classroom instruction, ensuring both practical expertise and theoretical understanding.
  • Originating from medieval craft guilds, the apprenticeship model has evolved but continues to emphasize mentorship and skill development under experienced practitioners.
  • Apprenticeships follow organized training plans, designed collaboratively by educators and industry, to help learners acquire trade-specific knowledge in progressive, achievable stages.

Experiential Learning

Experiential learning is an educational approach centred on learning through direct experience, reflection, and active participation. The theory combines theory and practice by placing learners in real-world contexts where they can apply knowledge and skills to gain deeper understanding (Gentry, 1990).

In this approach, learners are not passive recipients but active participants who shape their own learning journey. They engage in hands-on activities such as experiments, fieldwork, simulations, and other immersive experiences that help with direct interaction with the subject matter (Kolb, 2014). Core principles of experiential learning include active engagement, reflection, real-world application, problem-solving, critical thinking, collaboration, and teamwork. Learning unfolds as an iterative cycle of experience, reflection, and application, where each stage strengthens and refines understanding through continuous feedback and practice (Silberman, 2007).

The key points of Experiential Learning are:

  • Emphasizes hands-on activities, simulations, and real-world tasks that connect theory with practice.
  • Students take ownership of their learning, engaging in reflection, problem-solving, and critical thinking.
  • Involves repeated cycles of experience, reflection, and application, where feedback refines skills and understanding.

Inquiry-based Learning

Inquiry-based learning is an approach where students actively explore questions, problems, or challenges, taking charge of their learning through research, experimentation, and critical thinking (Friesen & Scott, 2013; Pedaste et al., 2015). Rather than passively receiving information, learners construct understanding by engaging directly with the inquiry process.

The approach begins with curiosity-driven or open-ended questions that guide investigation. Students gather evidence through research, experiments, observations, interviews, or fieldwork, analyzing and interpreting information to develop insights (Bayram et al., 2013). Critical thinking, problem-solving, and reasoning are central, while collaboration and discussion enhance communication and teamwork. Reflection encourages students to assess progress, connect new knowledge to prior understanding, and strengthen metacognitive skills. Teachers play a guiding role, supporting inquiry and asking questions that deepen comprehension without being the sole source of knowledge (Maaß & Doorman, 2013).

Inquiry-based learning applies across disciplines, allowing students to follow personal interests and make interdisciplinary connections. This approach encourages deeper conceptual understanding, enhances critical thinking, and develops the capacity to apply knowledge in real-world contexts.

The key points of Inquiry Based Learning are:

  • Inquiry-based learning engages students in asking questions, investigating, and constructing their own understanding.
  • It aligns with developing critical thinking, problem-solving, collaboration, communication, reflection, and metacognitive skills.
  • Teachers guide inquiry without giving all answers, and the approach can be applied across disciplines to deepen understanding and support real-world learning.

Early PBL Models

This section examines prominent PBL models implemented at universities worldwide, highlighting their structures, strategies, and impacts on student engagement, cognitive capabilities, and knowledge construction.

Aalborg University

Aalborg University, Denmark, has followed problem-based and project-organized teaching model since its inception in 1974 (Kolmos & Fink, 2004). This project-organized problem-solving model has the key components of literature, lectures, group studies, tutorials, field studies and experiments while the life cycle of the problem transits from problem analysis to problem solving ending with a documentation that reports the work carried out (Kolmos et al., 2004). The variants of the model are used across the courses where at least half the university credits and time is spent for the project work. The knowledge learned through the course work needs to be applied to the projects that are carried out. Along with pragmatic development, the central learning components of their model learning model has problem, content and the team (De Graaf & Kolmos, 2003). The usage of socio-cultural approach reduces the gap between the theory, technology, tools, language and the knowledge and participation in solving concrete and contextualized problems (Hernandez et al., 2015). The university promotes the activity by its UICEE centre dedicated for the PBL practices (Fink et al., 2002).

Republic Polytechic

Republic Polytechnic, Singapore follows the model of one day one problem. Students solve one problem which spans for an entire day with faculty as a facilitator. Known and to be known play a major role in the problem solving process (O'Grady et al., 2012). The problem definition template is split across what we know, what we don't know and what we need to find out. The phases of a day are divided into self-directed learning phases, meetings, reporting and reflections happening across a day's span. The learning is supported by appropriate physical infrastructure. The university has a dedicated centre to craft the problems for PBL sessions.

Stanford University

Stanford has a P5BL approach where the five Ps' stand for people, problem, process, product and project. The method was initiated in 1993 for inter-disciplinary integrated development strategies which familiarized the students to real world problem and instil a confidence to solve them (Fruchter, 1998). The key terms of the methodology used were cross-disciplinary, team interactions, scaffolding, etc. With the scope of students learning beyond the university walls, the students during the project time line play the roles of apprentice and the journeyman and the teams are mentored by professors and the industry practitioners. The process also supports reverse mentoring (Fruchter & Lewis, 2000). The team members are spread across the geographical regions with different time zones. The lab supports and mentors all the activities via the PBL Lab ((Fruchter & Lewis, 20001).

University of Delaware

With a set of faculty being trained, the University of Delaware introduced the problem based learning in the early 1990's. (Allen et al., 2003). PBL has been adapted in the first year setting (Duch, 2001). Exploring the role of technology has been an important part of our progress in developing PBL at Delaware (Watson, 2002). Over the years, PBL has been employed in various domains and experimented at several levels. The models employed majorly find inspiration from medical background.

University of Ulster

Ulster University has implemented PBL for several years, using workshops, seminars, and a dedicated PBL network to support its adoption, including online formats (Hack et al., 2015). PBL enhances students’ learning experiences, develops critical thinking, and prepares them for real-world challenges across fields such as health and social care, engineering, and business. Faculty receive training and support to design and deliver PBL curricula, equipping them to guide students effectively through the learning process. Courses incorporate authentic problem scenarios aligned with learning objectives, challenging students to integrate knowledge from multiple sources to develop solutions. Students work in small groups, sharing perspectives, engaging in discussions, and collaboratively addressing problems. Tutors provide guidance, clarify concepts, suggest resources, and encourage reflection on learning. Students evaluate their problem-solving processes, learning outcomes, and the transfer of skills to real-world contexts. Assessment includes individual and group evaluations, presentations, and written reports.

2.4 Evolution and Extensions of PBL

Evolution and Extensions of PBL

This section explores the evolution and extensions of PBL examining how learning models, historical developments, and contemporary variants have shaped its practice. It highlights the ways PBL has adapted over time, including changes in methodology, assessment, and application across different disciplines, and considers current approaches that demonstrate its flexibility and continued relevance in education.

Learning Models Related to PBL

This section explores how the related learning models concepts offer insights into how learners progress, navigate transitional stages, and integrate new knowledge with existing understanding. They highlight the importance of encountering challenges that push learners beyond their current capabilities, encouraging reflection, critical thinking, and adaptation.

Kolb’s Learning Cycle

Kolb's learning style theory, developed by David A. Kolb, is a model that describes how individuals perceive and process information during the learning process (Stice, 1987). According to Kolb, learning involves a cycle of four stages, each of which corresponds to a different learning style. The four stages are: concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation (Kolb, 2007). The phases can be started from anywhere but has to complete the one entire cycle for an effective learning process.

In the concrete experience stage, learners engage directly with an activity or situation, preferring hands-on involvement. Reflective observation involves stepping back to analyse experiences, identify patterns, and connect ideas, appealing to those who favour introspection. Abstract conceptualization allows learners to create concepts, theories, or models from their observations, interpreting experiences logically and theoretically. Active experimentation enables learners to apply concepts in practical situations, testing ideas and learning from outcomes.

According to Kolb, individuals have different learning styles, with each person tending to favour certain phases of the learning cycle over others. These styles reflect specific preferences and strengths in approaching learning and include converging, diverging, assimilating, and accommodating. Those with a converging style excel in abstract conceptualization and active experimentation, enjoying problem-solving, technical tasks, and practical applications. Diverging learners thrive in concrete experience and reflective observation, preferring observation, brainstorming, group work, and creative activities. Assimilating learners are strong in abstract conceptualization, favouring theoretical models, structured analysis, lectures, and readings. Accommodating learners excel in concrete experience and active experimentation, enjoying hands-on tasks, practical applications, and trial-and-error approaches. Classroom instruction can align activities with these learning styles while engaging all stages of Kolb’s cycle, ensuring a balanced and effective learning experience.

Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development

Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) is a concept developed by the psychologist Lev Vygotsky. It refers to the gap between a learner's current level of independent ability and their potential level of skill development with the assistance of a more knowledgeable other, such as a teacher, parent, or peer (Vygotsky, 2016). According to Vygotsky, learning does not occur only through individual exploration and discovery but is largely influenced by social interactions and guidance from others. The Zone of Proximal Development represents the range of tasks that a learner is not yet able to do independently but can accomplish with the guidance and support of someone more knowledgeable (Bruner, 1984).

The ZPD suggests that the most effective learning takes place when learners engage in activities that challenge them just beyond their current level of competence. It emphasizes the importance of scaffolding, which refers to the support and assistance provided by a knowledgeable person to help the learner bridge the gap between their current ability and the desired learning outcome. The role of the more knowledgeable other is to provide appropriate guidance, prompts, explanations, and examples to enable the learner to develop new skills or understanding. This support gradually decreases as the learner gains competence and is able to perform the task independently. Vygotsky believed that learning within the Zone of Proximal Development promotes cognitive development, as learners are exposed to more advanced ideas and concepts that they can internalize and integrate into their existing knowledge. It also encourages social interaction, collaboration, and the development of problem-solving and critical thinking skills (Sanders and Welk, 2005).

The Zone of Proximal Development highlights the importance of assessing learners' current abilities and tailoring instruction to their specific needs. By identifying and providing appropriate challenges and support, educators can facilitate optimal learning experiences and help learners reach their full potential (Chaiklin, 2003).

Liminal Spaces

Liminal space refers to a transitional or in-between state that engineering students experience during their educational journey. Liminal space can be seen as a period of ambiguity, uncertainty, and transformation where students are transitioning from being novices to becoming competent professionals in the field of engineering (Morgan, 2012). Engineering education often involves a shift from theoretical knowledge acquisition to the practical application of engineering principles. During this transition, students may find themselves in a liminal space where they are no longer beginners but have not yet fully developed the expertise and confidence of experienced engineers.

In the liminal space, engineering students may encounter challenges such as uncertainty, identity formation, skill development, and integration of Knowledge. Navigating the liminal space in engineering education requires support and guidance from educators, mentors, and industry professionals (Phillips, 2017). Providing opportunities for experiential learning, mentorship programs, and professional development activities can help students gain confidence, bridge the gap between theory and practice, and develop a professional identity. Embracing the liminal space as a valuable learning experience can empower engineering students to grow, adapt, and thrive in their journey towards becoming competent and successful engineers (McCartney et al., 2009).

Mental Models

Sources to construct mental models are the learner’s ability to construct models in an inductive manner, either from a set of basic components of world knowledge or from analogous models that they already possesses, everyday observations of the outside world and other people’s explanations (Johnson-Laird, 1983). There are three different opportunities to enhance exploratory learning and problem solving through instruction aiming at: Self-organized discovery learning, externally guided discovery learning and receptive meaningful learning.

According to Johnson-Laird, mental models are formed through perception, comprehension, and reasoning, serving as internal representations of objects, events, processes, or situations that capture essential features and relationships of the external world. They simplify complex information by focusing on core aspects while abstracting unnecessary details, enabling individuals to make predictions, draw inferences, and reason logically by manipulating and updating these representations. Mental models strive for coherence and consistency, adjusting when new information challenges existing understanding, and are shaped by the context in which they are formed, allowing multiple models for the same situation. Constructed actively by individuals, mental models reflect personal knowledge, experiences, and cognitive processes rather than directly replicating reality.

Threshold Concepts

Threshold concepts represent core ideas within a discipline that, once grasped, fundamentally reshape a learner’s understanding and engagement with the subject. They serve as conceptual gateways, allowing learners to integrate and synthesize knowledge that was previously inaccessible, often requiring confrontation with “troublesome” or counterintuitive material that challenges existing cognitive frameworks. These concepts are inherently integrative, connecting diverse aspects of a discipline, and their acquisition is typically irreversible, producing lasting shifts in perception and reasoning. Mastery of threshold concepts supports the development of disciplinary expertise, fosters deep and meaningful learning, and strengthens the ability to apply knowledge adaptively across varied contexts and complex problem-solving scenarios (Cousin, 2006).

Meyer and Land (2003) describe threshold concepts as potentially troublesome gateways that can change how learners understand and practise within a discipline. Liminality names the unsettled in-between period that may accompany this change.

Evolution of PBL (1990-2022)

This subsection presents the evolution of the domain by presenting the major research works published from the year 1990 to 2022. The papers were selected from Google scholar for the mentioned years sorted by relevance and depending on the number of citations, appearing within the first page of search results and for which the article is available for download. Text books with related titles and resources appearing with the title 'problem based learning' only were not given the first priority as text books are large and complex to comprehend in short and titles matching exactly the keyword were not selected as they were too generic for the study. The search term used for each year was 'problem based learning' with no prefix or suffix keywords. The reading list was prepared during January of 2023.

The knowledge by far acquired by the students, motivation to study and explore further, process of analysis and meeting educational objectives have been major criteria used to construct the problems in PBL (Majoor et al., 1990). A comprehensive series of four faculty development programs were introduced to train the facilitators on PBL methods leading to a significant impact on a successful shift to use the methods (Grand'Maison & Des Marchais, 1991). A psychological basis and a study for review of evidence deliberated on the aspects of motivation, problem-solving skills, recalling information, using knowledge to solve problems and self-directed learners in PBL (Norman & Schmidt, 1992). A study analysed the effectiveness of problems used in PBL by measuring the number of objectives met for the course and the student generated learning issues (Dolmans et al., 1993).

The availability of reading resources, course objectives, lecture notes etc. have an impact on students and make them better self-directed learners. These clues and directions help students to take decisions on what and how to study (Dolmans & Schmidt, 1994). With the traditional method of teaching, a student cannot begin their professional life with the assumption n that they have acquired all the knowledge to solve the real world problems. With PBL, when value for the information is known, encourages the development of professional skills while being a student. With the increased student enjoyment and encouraging team working environment, PBL helps one acquire the problem solving skills (Wood et al., 1995).

In a group problem solving, cognitive and metacognitive processes have been analysed using verbal communication and thinking processes (De Grave et al., 1996). Working together in group is a central element of a PBL and a successful team needs to have several skills. These skill set include decision making with a consent, discussion and deliberation skill, skills to maintain team, skill to manage conflicts and team leadership skills (Peterson, 1997). The learning support systems for the PBL have been discussed. Scaffolding is essential to motivate students to involve in deep thinking, appropriate materials to be covered for ill-structured problems and support for decisions and actions in problem solving process. Scaffolding also plays a role in designing methods to become a life-long learner to adapt to the changes that are happening at the phenomenal rate, design of assessment methods and starting PBL at early stages so at to provide sufficient time to learn and master the information gathering process (Greening, 1998).

In a PBL process the role of facilitator changes and evolves over time. A novice PBL student might need more guidance as compared to the mature students. A tutor in PBL is a cognitive expert whose role varies in student tutoring, as content expert and as student evaluator. An ideal tutor is the one who is flexible, understands and differentiates the role nature, is sensitive to the student's learning requirements and well-informed about the syllabus (Neville, 1999). Learning is a process of finding out knowledge that is not known yet. PBL has its own lived experience for staff and students based on the nature of the PBL form selected and learning context, learner identity and learning in relation play a major role as well. PBL and its complexity as an approach must make sense to its stake holders personally, pedagogically and interactionally learn with complexity and through ambiguity (Savin-Baden, 2000).

Research proves that PBL is effective to be one prepared for the future than traditional learning methods and problem solving skills are more important than just the memory recall process. The landscape of PBL covers and needs administration on organisational support, the study and nature of discipline in which it has to be used, tutor preparation and development (Savin-Baden, 2001). For designing a task in PBL one has to determine the form of PBL environment, focus on target learning outcomes, determine the learning activities associated with the task and present the task to students. As a formative assessment it's essential for a tutor to provide both written and verbal feedback along with quality of their response in association with achievement of task-related learning outcomes. Summative assessments can be accordingly carried out with team and individual grading (Forsythe, 2002). PBL has its principles structured around contextual learning and courses can evolve around that. PBL helps to construct an atmosphere where students want to learn, take decisions, be accountable for their learning and be successful. Designing assessment methods in PBL need substantial and continuing effort (Hillman, 2003).

Studies show that student's cultural beliefs and folklore are the sources of inspiration for problems and questions. The categories of questions which students ask or discuss individually or collaboratively are driven by various factors like advertisements and media, personal encounters, concerns arising from friends and family, observations made looking at others and queries from the knowledge of previous classes. The kinds of questions asked have a major impact on the knowledge construction process. Questions asked individually are usually beliefs and misconceptions, basic information, explanations, and imagined scenarios. Questions asked collaboratively leads a pathway to learning and extents to which these get answered are important to sustain interest in the project (Chin & Chia, 2004). Tutor has a prominent role in the delivery of the PBL process. Studies indicate that students prefer expert tutors as they enable to identify relevant learning issues over non-expert tutors. The method promotes students as active learners and makes them responsible for their own learning process. Students undergoing PBL method as compared to the traditional teaching have more focus towards gaining an ability to apply knowledge, the development of their critical thinking skills, changing their conceptions of the topics studied and having a more relativistic view on knowledge (Dochy et al., 2005).

Studies indicate that using PBL not only affects the PBL-related competencies in the interpersonal and cognitive domains, but it also has impact on the work-related skills such as writing reports, presenting papers, research etc. that are essential for success in professional practice. Students engaging in PBL activities produce new ideas, help each other, work towards being productive, learn to work independently, plan the works adequately and be efficient and also learn to work under pressure (Schmidt et al., 2006). PBL focuses largely on deep learning then surface learning. PBL is not a one-time change. The process has to constantly renew and the success of curriculum innovation in the long run depends on the ability of the faculty to adapt the educational method to suit its own specific needs (De Graaff & Kolmos, 2007). PBL has influence of Pragmatism (approach that uses theories and beliefs for the policies) and Structuralism (focus is on relationship rather than on individual). While the learner in PBL seeks the study materials to solve the problems, the influence and relation of proactive and reactive learning methods need to be researched yet. For a topic that is constantly evolving it is essential to learn in the context and not only the specifics of it (Chen, 2008).

PBL evolves with personalized forms of knowledge. The set of rules designed to ensure convergence of teaching practice and equality of opportunity to students evolve with the community of practice and facilitator. Teachers usually struggle in varying degrees while practicing complex issues and how the method has to be practiced. The team practicing PBL needs to have a vision discuss and form a community of practice and strive to maintain open discussions about teaching and learning (Spronken-Smith & Harland, 2009). PBL models vary on the fronts of program structures, format of problems, method of implementation and monitoring and assessments. From a subject level to institution level, various PBL models are designed to deliver basic to professional skills during the degree program including organizing the initial years into thematic nodules. The problems are usually given in the form of scenarios, case-based, events, product prototypes, laboratory sheets etc where either students undergo the set process or design their own meeting the set standards. The team sizes and tutor involve varies in different contexts. Most PBL methods focus on accessing the process skills, which are assessed individually and in teams (Masek & Yamin, 2010).

Research shows that the learning in each PBL phase is cumulative, and is also strongly influenced by the earlier phase. This provides the required support for the PBL cycle of problem analysis, self-directed learning, and a subsequent reporting phase (Yew et al., 2011). The tips for constructing cases include establishing writing teams, identifying the learning objectives for the case, constructing a template for the case, validating the authenticity, integrity and logical flow of the case, designing the case study according to student learning needs, writing a case with engaging trigger, designing images for the cases, ensuring that the deductive approach is applicable, ensuring that the learning objectives are well presented in the case, student engagement with the case, having a tutor guide for each case and reviewing and encouraging the feedback (Azer et al., 2012). PBL has also been effective when delivered in hybrid mode promoting active and self-directed learning. A network of faculty must work towards the changing needs adapting to the need of the hour skills, for the reason that a good curriculum is never finished (Armstrong, 2013).

Research classifies PBL into several constellations namely PBL for knowledge management, for learning through activity, project–led, for practical ability, for design based problems, for critical understanding, for multimodal reasoning, collaborative distributed, and for transformation and social reform (Savin-Baden, 2014). Literatures describe on how the critical and creative thinking skills can be embedded into instruction design. Students in a PBL session participate in an active process to create creative solutions toward the problem from their experiences which involves discovering new knowledge and using the pre-requisites. Learner analysis, context analysis, organization of instructional objectives, development of instruction strategies, implementation process and assessment techniques – all of them need a proper blend of creative and critical thinking skills (Birgili, 2015). Implementing PBL in technical sciences has its own challenges considering the nature of courses and the degree program. However, the experimentations carried out in the regards have found to be effective on smaller scales and based on nature of courses (Rodríguez González & Fernández Batanero, 2016). PBL when explored in virtual learning environment was found to be effective than traditional group when the principles of instructional systems design and systems approach were integrated into the design and assessment phases of model development for a communication arts course Phungsuk et al., 2017).

Studies have compared PBL with traditional using pre-test and post-test methods. Studies confirm that PBL can improve the mathematical problem solving capabilities and self-confidence (Hendriana et al., 2018). PBL has also been used as an effort to improve student learning outcomes (Malmia et al., 2019). Researches also conclude that the application of problem based learning has more positive effect than direct instruction in terms of critical thinking ability in solving mathematical problems (Umar et al., 2020).

Through the literature review PBL Frameworks have been proposed with three major elements. One being that PBL gives the context of an authentic engineering practice, two that it supports learners' agency in the process of becoming professionals, and finally it gives opportunities to work and learn simultaneously (Mann et al., 2021). The recent study results have indicated an improvement in students' cognitive learning outcomes following differentiated learning within the PBL model (Dalila et al., 2022).

Contemporary Models and Variants

Engineering education’s shift from subject-based instruction to contextually rich learning is exemplified by the Aalborg Model of Problem-Oriented Project Learning (POPBL). At Aalborg University, students tackle real-world, sustainability-focused problems that demand technical proficiency, systems thinking, and social awareness (Lehmann et al., 2008). Evidence shows POPBL enhances students’ ability to identify and address multifaceted societal problems through interdisciplinary approaches. Higher education contributes to advancing Education for Sustainable Development by embedding elements such as lifelong learning, interdisciplinary, systems thinking, partnerships, multicultural perspectives, and empowerment. POPBL offers a framework to integrate these dimensions through an action research approach, despite the challenges of uniting them within a single course (Yasin & Rahman, 2011). There have been discussions on the challenges of implementing POPBL, particularly around resistance to change, the need for clear vision, and actionable planning across institutional, group, and individual levels. These discussions also address staff behavior, organizational change, and long-term transitions, showing how collaboration and gradual adaptation can lead to successful adoption of new educational models (Moesby, 2004). There have been growing efforts to design programs that prepare entrepreneurs to solve complex business problems through digital technologies. A blended learning model using POPBL and an integrated-modular framework in the DTBI program has shown positive outcomes, with students achieving strong performance, satisfaction, and evidence of enhanced learning (Chongdarakul et al., 2021)

In computer science education, Case-Based Learning (CBL) has gained traction in courses like compiler design. Real-life case narratives paired with project tasks enable learners to apply classroom concepts practically. Studies report improvements in engagement, critical thinking, communication, and teamwork resulting from this method (Kundra & Sureka, 2016). At two medical schools, a curricular shift from Problem-Based Learning (PBL) to CBL showed that both students and faculty overwhelmingly preferred the more structured CBL format. Respondents highlighted that CBL reduced tangents and busy-work while offering clearer opportunities for applying clinical skills, whereas PBL was valued mainly for encouraging self-directed learning and participation. There are findings that suggest in dense curricula requiring efficient use of time, CBL may provide a more practical and effective alternative to traditional PBL, though its long-term impact on physician competency remains uncertain (Srinivasan et al., 2007). CBL in undergraduate nursing programs was found to cultivate three key learning practices: recognizing clinical particulars, interpreting patient data for decisions, and reflection. These practices translated into four professional attributes: strong clinical knowledge, diverse thinking approaches, a developing professional identity, and professional caring. The findings suggest that CBL better prepares nursing students for workplace expectations compared to traditional curricula, highlighting its potential to bridge gaps between education and professional practice (Kantar & Massouh, 2015).

Hybrid or blended PBL models integrate problem-based inquiry into traditional curricula, balancing conceptual instruction with applied learning. While explicit engineering-based hybrid PBL research is limited, studies in similar fields reveal hybrid models enhance autonomy, reinforce understanding, and support deeper engagement when the theoretical base is scaffold alongside problem-solving experiences. For example, hybrid PBL has demonstrated improved performance and self-directed learning in medical education and holds promise for transfer to STEM fields where foundational knowledge needs practical grounding (Lavado-Anguera et al., 2024). The hybrid-PBL model, combining lectures with tutor-less problem-based learning, enabled medical students in large classroom settings to achieve higher scores in essay and case-analysis assessments compared to traditional lecture-based learning. Students favoured the hybrid approach, reporting gains in critical thinking, access to resources, and more engaging learning experiences, suggesting it as a viable alternative for medical education in China (Lian & He, 2013). The hybrid PBL model in nursing education demonstrated clear advantages over conventional lecture-based teaching, improving higher-order cognitive performance, instructional efficiency, and reducing mental effort among students. Compared to pure PBL, the hybrid model showed sustained benefits on delayed assessments, highlighting its potential as a practical and effective approach, particularly in contexts with limited teaching resources (Salari et al., 2018).

Problem-centred approaches such as POPBL, CBL, and hybrid PBL demonstrate how flexible learning models can bridge theory and practice across disciplines. POPBL immerses students in real-world, project-based contexts that build technical knowledge, systems thinking, and collaborative skills while addressing sustainability challenges. CBL provides structured, case-driven learning that strengthens reasoning, decision-making, and professional readiness in both technical and healthcare fields. Hybrid PBL combines lectures with inquiry-driven learning, offering balance between conceptual grounding and active problem solving. Collectively, these models highlight the adaptability of PBL principles, showing their capacity to enhance engagement, autonomy, and higher-order skills in diverse educational settings. The three adaptations: POPBL, CBL, and hybrid PBL demonstrate how PBL can be flexibly shaped to meet domain requirements, linking theory with practice through authentic, student-centred learning.

2.5 Applications of PBL

Application of PBL across Domains

PBL has been applied across diverse disciplines, offering rich insights into its adaptability and impact. Exploring its use in computing alongside fields such as medicine, law, management, etc. helps identify transferable strategies and practices that can strengthen CSE education and enhance problem-solving, collaboration, and knowledge application.

PBL in Computing

There are characteristic matches between computing and problem based learning. Computing is, for the most part, is also problem driven. Life-long learning is a necessity due to the rapidly and continually changing nature of the industry. Practitioners must constantly update their skills and competencies in order to keep abreast of new technology, the project group is the predominant mode of operation within the industry and computing crosses discipline boundaries (Ellis et al., 1998). The 3P model of a teaching and learning system (Biggs & Tang, 2011) which focuses on three major points: presage - which is before the learning takes place, process – which is during the learning and product – which is the learning outcome. The 3P model is useful at macro level which allows focusing on factors before the trigger is introduced to the students, the process of PBL and the outcomes at the end of the case studies (Beaumont et al., 2004). Though the experience with PBL has been positive, the penetration into the curricula has been shallow. The process of adaptation though is increasing and is ad-hoc and mostly random, calls for a systematic approach (O’Grady, 2012).

Several challenges have been identified when PBL is applied to computing courses. A survey carried out on PBL methods in computing between 1997 to 2011, which assesses 52 primary studies, lists the following as the challenges: application of the methodology, difficulties in disciplines, knowledge of methodology, time, problem kinds and variants, restriction on the methodology, knowledge of students, dissemination of knowledge, use of other technology, the interest of students in computer science and difficulty in the objective reached and results (Oliveira et al., 2013). Another study of 102 primary studies reports that PBL can be effectively applied for software and systems engineering, stimulates motivation and engagement of studies, fosters hard and soft skills, and needs good planning and continuous monitoring. The paper further mentions the challenges listing out the concern of the problem proposition, the high cost of a learning environment compatible with the reality of the labour market, elaboration of non-traditional content, the definition of educational objectives, and its monitoring through assessment processes (dos Santos et al., 2020).

Numerous research efforts have been made in teaching programming courses in computer science. Foundations of programming have been deliberated using several methods (Mitchell, 1996). Semantics of C programming has been studied in the regards for PBL (Gurevich & Huggins, 1992). Program analysis and specializations have been carried out (Andersen, 1994). Educational ontologies for C programming have been developed (Sosnovsky & Gavrilova, 2006). C programming has been researched from various dimensions and evolving approaches have been reflected (King, 1996). A four step method, where software packages, technology and tools were introduced to teach C programming effectively (Budny et al., 2002). Research and reflections have been discussed on teaching of C programming language with the design aspects (Gao et al., 2015). Methods for adaptive, engaging, and explanatory visualizations have been created for delivery of C programming course (Brusilovsky & Spring, 2004). Gamified android apps have been developed as well (Talingdan & Llanda, 2019).

PBL has also been researched to design effective methods to teach programming. Of the many available learning strategies that are available, the popular few are: problem based, puzzle based, pair programming, pre-recorded lectures and game themed (Mohorovicic & Strcic, 2011). Comprehension capability is a major factor that decides the concept complexity (Milne & Rowe, 2002). The seven steps method with adaptations has been devised to integrate PBL and programming (Nuutila et al., 2005). Tools like RoboCode with non-prescriptive approach have been developed integrating PBL experience (O'Kelly & Gibson, 2006). Statistical tools like SPSS have been used to analyze the difficulties in learning introductory programming so as to aid the educators in developing the alternative and effective strategies (Looi & Seyal, 2014). PBL method has been combined with flipped classroom mode for programming course resulting in creating enjoyable learning experience (Chis et al., 2018). Self-learning, active exploration, and mutual cooperation are recognized as essential elements in the learning process (Peng, 2010). As studied, when the focus shifts to open-ended or real-world problems, their interpretation takes on unique dimensions within programming education. Such problems require careful adaptation to the domain, where abstract concepts, logic, and computational thinking must be translated into concrete coding practices and solutions (Nuutila et al., 2008). This alignment ensures that learners practice technical skills and engage in authentic problem solving that mirrors real professional contexts.

This part covers the usage of PBL in various engineering domains like computers, electrical, mechanical, bio, mathematics, civil etc. The pedagogy has been used in collaboration with numerous courses to achieve the course objectives.

2.6 Computational Thinking

Computational Thinking

Computational Thinking (CT) has emerged as a foundational concept in computer science education, shaping how problems are approached and solved. This section explores the origins of CT and clarifies its core elements. It also examines its role in curricula, highlighting its importance for engineers and its broader relevance as a problem-solving approach across disciplines.

Origins and Definitions

Computational Thinking is commonly described through four key constituents: abstraction, decomposition, pattern recognition, and algorithms (Wing, 2006). Research has pointed to the need for clearer definitions of the terminology associated with this process (Selby & Woollard, 2013). Over time, various studies have examined the close relationship between computational thinking and problem solving. Experimental work has evaluated the effectiveness of integrating PBL and computational thinking within programming (Chen, 2017), while other studies have explored how combining PBL with computer science influences learning outcomes and student attitudes (Kwon et al., 2021). The broader links between computational thinking and computer science continue to be actively discussed and refined.

According to Jeannette Wing (2006), Computational Thinking (CT) is “the thought processes involved in formulating problems and their solutions so that the solutions are represented in a form that can be effectively carried out by an information-processing agent.” She emphasized that CT goes beyond programming. It includes abstraction, decomposition, pattern recognition, and algorithmic thinking, and is a fundamental skill for everyone, not just computer scientists.

Core Elements of CT

CT is grounded in four essential elements: abstraction, decomposition, pattern recognition, and algorithmic thinking. Together, these form a way of approaching problems systematically and constructing solutions that can be applied across diverse contexts.

Abstraction refers to the process of simplifying complexity by focusing on the essential features of a problem while ignoring unnecessary details. It allows learners to represent problems in manageable forms, making it possible to design solutions that are broadly applicable rather than tied to a single instance. Decomposition involves breaking down a large or complex problem into smaller, more manageable parts. This step makes the problem less overwhelming and enables incremental progress. Each component can then be understood and addressed before integrating solutions into a coherent whole. Pattern recognition is the ability to identify similarities, differences, and trends across problems or data. Recognizing patterns helps in predicting outcomes, reusing previous solutions, and developing general strategies for related situations. Algorithmic thinking is the practice of outlining a clear, step-by-step process to solve a problem. It emphasizes precision, logical order, and efficiency, ensuring that solutions can be consistently reproduced and implemented, whether by humans or machines.

These four elements, when combined, capture the essence of Computational Thinking: an approach that enables structured analysis, effective problem solving, and adaptable solutions across disciplines.

CT in Education and CSE Curriculum

CT is increasingly recognized as a fundamental competency in the 21st century, yet its precise definition and effective pedagogical integration remain subjects of scholarly debate. While often associated with computer science, CT outdoes coding and represents a broader problem-solving framework applicable across disciplines (Grover & Pea, 2013). Wing’s (2006) influential definition positioned CT as “the thought processes involved in formulating problems and their solutions so that the solutions are represented in a form that can be effectively carried out by an information-processing agent.” Her framing emphasized the universality of CT, proposing it as a basic skill for all learners. However, later research has critiqued this definition for being too general and not sufficiently grounded in pedagogy or assessment.

Selby and Woollard (2013) argued that CT requires a clearer articulation of its core constructs: abstraction, decomposition, pattern recognition and algorithmic thinking so that educators can design effective instructional interventions. Their work suggested that Wing’s vision, while influential, left significant ambiguity around how CT could be operationalized in classroom practice. Similarly, Grover and Pea (2013) emphasized the need to view CT not only as a set of practices but also as a developmental process involving cognitive skills, dispositions, and habits of mind.

Recent research has expanded these perspectives by examining CT as both a cognitive skill set and a pedagogical challenge. Studies focus on how learners engage in decomposition, recognize patterns, abstract essential features, and design algorithms to navigate complex problem spaces. Other research explores how active learning strategies, such as inquiry-based and project-based learning, can be leveraged to develop CT competencies (Yadav et al., 2017). This includes investigations into the effectiveness of unplugged activities, visual programming tools, and text-based coding environments in cultivating CT.

Another strand of research addresses the transferability of CT skills, questioning whether abilities developed in computing contexts extend meaningfully to domains such as mathematics, science, engineering, or the humanities. This challenge has also brought attention to assessment: moving beyond surface-level coding proficiency toward capturing deeper conceptual understanding and problem-solving strategies requires new methods of evaluation (Weintrop et al., 2016).

For engineers, CT is particularly significant because it aligns closely with the problem-solving mind-set required in the profession. Engineering tasks often demand decomposition of complex systems, recognition of patterns in technical processes, abstraction of essential design requirements, and algorithmic approaches to developing efficient solutions. Embedding CT within engineering curricula therefore strengthens students’ ability to approach open-ended problems systematically, bridging theory and practice in professional contexts.

CT has become integral to Computer Science and Engineering (CSE) curricula, embedded across foundational and advanced courses. In introductory programming, CT is nurtured through decomposition of problems into functions, pattern recognition across coding exercises, and abstraction using data types and control structures (Grover & Pea, 2013). Courses on algorithms and data structures strengthen algorithmic thinking by emphasizing efficiency analysis, generalization, and systematic reasoning about computational complexity (Shute et al., 2017). In software engineering, CT is evident in modular system design, iterative development, and structured debugging processes that require abstraction and decomposition at scale. Advanced areas such as artificial intelligence, networks, and systems design extend CT into ill-structured problem domains, demanding higher levels of abstraction and adaptive problem-solving strategies (Weintrop et al., 2016).

Embedding CT in this layered manner ensures that students experience it not as an isolated competency but as a unifying lens through which core CSE concepts are taught and applied. This approach aligns with the professional realities of engineering practice, where CT is essential for designing scalable systems, managing complexity, and transferring solutions across technical domains. Scholars argue that such integration enhances students’ ability to bridge theory and practice, preparing them for both academic progression and industry demands (Yadav et al., 2017).

2.7 Integrating PBL and CT

Integrating PBL and CT

PBL and CT represent two influential pedagogical constructs that converge on the central theme of problem solving. PBL situates learning within authentic, real-world contexts that promote inquiry, collaboration, and critical reflection, while CT provides structured strategies such as abstraction, decomposition, pattern recognition, and algorithmic design for addressing complex challenges systematically. Examining their conceptual alignment, existing frameworks, and prior research offers a foundation for understanding how their integration can enhance knowledge construction, self-directed learning, and the transfer of problem-solving skills across disciplinary boundaries.

Conceptual Links

PBL and CT share a common concern with developing learners’ capacity to engage with complex problems, but they do so from complementary perspectives. PBL situates learning within real-world contexts, where problems are open-ended, interdisciplinary, and embedded in authentic practice (Barrows, 1986). Such problems rarely have a single correct answer; instead, they demand collaboration, critical reflection, and iterative exploration. In contrast, CT provides structured cognitive strategies for addressing complexity. Through processes such as abstraction, decomposition, pattern recognition, and algorithmic design, learners are equipped to approach ill-structured problems with systematic reasoning (Wing, 2006).

These approaches together form a mutually reinforcing framework. PBL ensures that learners are immersed in meaningful contexts where knowledge is actively constructed, while CT supplies the analytical tools for organizing and solving the underlying challenges. Research suggests that integrating CT within PBL environments strengthens students’ ability to translate domain knowledge into practical solutions, particularly in computing and engineering education (Grover & Pea, 2013; Kwon et al., 2021). The alignment of PBL’s authenticity with CT’s structured problem-solving process thus promotes deeper engagement, transferable skills, and a more holistic development of problem-solving competence that extends across disciplines and professional domains. Frameworks and Prior Research

The integration of PBL and CT has been studied extensively, as both converge on the aim of developing transferable problem-solving skills. PBL situates learning in authentic, real-world contexts where learners collaboratively engage with open-ended challenges (Barrows, 1986), while CT contributes structured approaches such as abstraction, decomposition, pattern recognition, and algorithmic design (Wing, 2006). Together, they provide a complementary framework in which PBL supplies meaningful contexts and CT supplies systematic processes, particularly valuable in computing and engineering education.

There is evidence that PBL can act as a facilitator of CT, since the inquiry and collaboration central to PBL create natural opportunities for developing CT competencies, particularly in higher education where students transition from analogue schooling into digitally intensive professional settings (Jonasen & Gram-Hansen, 2019). The combination is also seen to support computational empowerment, enabling learners to apply computational methods critically and constructively to complex problems.

Empirical findings reinforce this view. A meta-analysis of 31 experiments and quasi-experiments showed that project-based learning significantly improves CT-related competencies, especially algorithmic thinking, collaboration, and problem solving, with stronger effects observed in middle school contexts (Zhang et al., 2024). Similar improvements have been reported in secondary education, where integrated PBL with flipped classrooms enhanced decomposition, abstraction, and pattern recognition, though algorithmic thinking developed more gradually (Fitrah et al., 2025). These findings highlight the need for intentional scaffolding when embedding CT into PBL environments.

Classroom-based studies further illustrate these effects. Integrating CT within PBL-based programming instruction improved analytical reasoning (Chen, 2017), while PBL activities in computer science courses enhanced CT competencies and improved motivation and attitudes toward programming (Kwon et al., 2021). There is a caution, however, that PBL by itself may not guarantee measurable CT gains, particularly in unplugged activities, unless explicit links are drawn between task design and CT constructs (Moreno-Palma et al., 2024).

Broader reviews echo these concerns. Strategies such as visualization tools, scaffolding, collaborative structures, and localized contexts have been identified as effective for integration, but the absence of a comprehensive guiding framework remains a challenge (Saad & Zainudin, 2024). Shute et al. (2017) describe computational thinking as a reusable problem-solving foundation and identify facets including decomposition, abstraction, algorithm design, debugging, iteration, and generalisation.

The application of PBL and CT extends across disciplines. Interdisciplinary work shows that their combination promotes flexible problem solving across technical and reflective domains, while CT-infused PBL has enabled learners in the humanities to transfer structured reasoning into interpretive problem spaces (Rahmawati, 2022). In engineering education, embedding CT within PBL contexts has been shown to enhance students’ capacity to handle ill-structured design challenges while strengthening collaboration and self-directed learning (Li, 2017).

Taken together, this body of work suggests that while PBL naturally supports elements of CT, deliberate instructional design is essential for robust outcomes. PBL offers authenticity and collaboration, whereas CT provides systematic reasoning strategies that improve efficiency and generalizability. Their integration strengthens knowledge construction, fosters higher-order reasoning, and prepares learners for the complex, interdisciplinary challenges characteristic of computing and engineering education.

Potential for Knowledge Construction

The integration of PBL and CT creates a unique learning space where problems act as catalysts and structured reasoning provides pathways to solutions. Rather than treating knowledge as something to be consumed, learners construct it through cycles of inquiry, organization, and application. The open-ended nature of PBL ensures that learning remains grounded in authentic contexts, while CT offers tools to navigate complexity, allowing students to recognize patterns, abstract essential elements, and design processes that can be applied beyond the immediate problem. This interplay transforms problems from isolated tasks into opportunities for building transferable understanding.

At the same time, the integration encourages learners to become self-managers of their learning journey. PBL requires autonomy in setting directions and seeking information, while CT adds the discipline of structuring that autonomy into effective strategies. This dual demand cultivates self-directed learners who are able to acquire knowledge and also translate it across domains. Knowledge transfer emerges naturally, since CT equips learners with generalizable reasoning skills, and PBL ensures those skills are repeatedly tested against varied, real-world contexts. Together, they prepare students for dynamic environments where adaptability, critical thinking, and lifelong learning are essential.

PBL supplies the context: open-ended, authentic problems that situate knowledge within meaningful experiences. CT supplies the structure: systematic strategies such as abstraction, decomposition, and algorithmic design that guide how learners make sense of complexity. When combined, context and structure converge to promote knowledge construction, as students are challenged to explore, to organize and to articulate their reasoning in ways that lead to deeper conceptual understanding.

2.8 Knowledge Construction

Knowledge Construction as Thematic Lens

Knowledge construction (KC) refers to the active and iterative process through which learners build, refine, and reorganize their understanding. It is not a passive reception of information but an effort to create meaning through inquiry, reflection, and interaction. Piaget (1952) frames KC as the restructuring of mental models through assimilation and accommodation, while Vygotsky (1978) emphasizes its social dimension, where dialogue and cultural tools mediate the co-construction of meaning. Extending these perspectives, Scardamalia and Bereiter (2006) describe KC as a communal and intentional effort to improve ideas, with learners treating knowledge as improvable and open to collective advancement. Across these accounts, KC is positioned as cognitive and social, shaped by individual reasoning and collaborative engagement.

Within PBL, KC is central. Real-world, ill-structured problems act as triggers that compel learners to activate prior knowledge, identify gaps, and construct new understanding through cycles of inquiry and reflection. Discussion and negotiation within groups and individually further refine this process, enabling learners to test hypotheses, articulate reasoning, and integrate diverse perspectives. Research on PBL demonstrates that such environments shift emphasis from memorization to conceptual integration, making knowledge more meaningful and durable (Hmelo-Silver, 2004).

CT complements KC by introducing structured strategies that shape how knowledge is constructed and extended. Decomposition helps learners break problems into manageable units, abstraction emphasizes essential features, pattern recognition connects across experiences, and algorithmic thinking organizes reasoning into systematic processes (Wing, 2006). These strategies ensure that the knowledge constructed in PBL contexts is relevant to the immediate task and organized in ways that support generalization and transfer. KC thus becomes the point of convergence: PBL provides the authentic context for inquiry, while CT provides the scaffolds that make constructed knowledge systematic, adaptable, and applicable across domains.

Positioning KC as the thematic lens in this framework highlights its integrative function. Problems serve as catalysts, self-directed learning emerges as learners regulate their inquiry, and transfer occurs as constructed knowledge is re-applied in new contexts. KC, therefore, is an outcome and the mechanism through which PBL and CT together enable problem solving, autonomy, and lifelong adaptability.
