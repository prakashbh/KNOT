9.1 Course design and data sources

The course map links how concepts were taught to evidence of learning

The course-delivery map assigns a teaching mode, a planned difficulty level, and lab availability to each of the 37 DAA concepts. Refined account of the teaching modes

Intuition. The lesson begins by making the need for an idea understandable. Students are first given a small story or real-world situation and a sense of what led people to the problem. The mathematical or social roots of the idea can be made visible before the formal concept is introduced. In the efficiency sequence, a real-world linear-search case provides the starting point. Students then count operations, identify the critical operations, and consider what efficiency means in theory and in practice.

Intuition and application problems. This mode adds a deliberate range of applications to the intuitive entry point. Students encounter why an idea matters and where it can be used before the formal concept is named and developed. The course map assigns this mode to Space and Time Efficiency problems. Its purpose is to give the concept several meaningful anchors, rather than presenting it as a definition without a reason for learning it.

Intuition and storytelling. Here a narrative carries the initial intuition. The teaching notes describe Towers of Hanoi through the story of monks at Kashi Vishwanath and a Planet of the Apes example involving Caesar. The story gives students an image and context to think with before they reason through the puzzle.

Traditional. In this mode, the instructor introduces the concept directly, then develops it through worked examples and applications. The notes distinguish this sequence from beginning with an open problem or activity. Direct explanation is one part of the course design, not evidence that students were never asked to apply or reason about the concept afterward.

Iterative Problem. Students meet a problem, examine where an approach becomes limited, and use that limitation to motivate the next idea. The tree sequence begins with a binary search tree and the problem of skewness. The class then considers alternatives, including 2-3 trees and red-black trees, and the role of rotations in balancing. The concepts build from one another, so each new structure answers a difficulty that became visible in the preceding one.

Problem. This is the fuller problem-based learning experience described in the notes. Students begin with a problem that creates uncertainty, connect it to what they already know, and work in groups to develop a solution while the instructor facilitates. For DFS and BFS, students consider a drawn graph or matrix and ask what changes when traversal uses a stack or a queue. The order of traversal becomes something they can reason out from the problem, rather than a rule to memorise first.

Activity and Problem. A physical activity makes the process observable, after which a problem gives students a reason to explain and improve it. For sorting, students manipulate paper chits and notice how the items move. The instructor then connects those actions to Bubble Sort and invites comparison and optimisation. The activity is not the endpoint; it provides shared experience that can be represented, discussed and formalised.

Taken together, these modes describe different starting points and paths through a lesson. A class may begin with context, narrative, direct explanation, a problem, or a physical experience. The common movement in the examples is toward making the concept visible, explaining why it is needed, and connecting it to formal reasoning or application. The mode map and notes document this course’s design choices. They do not show that every lesson followed an identical sequence or that one mode was more effective than another.

Case studies and labs made the thinking visible

The case-study and laboratory sheets show how students were introduced to problems and what they were asked to do with them. Many prompts describe a situation first, leave room for students to decide how to represent it, and then ask them to explain a choice, test an approach, or reflect on what they learned. Three examples show this design particularly clearly.

Read retrospectively, these materials help explain how the KNOT philosophy emerged from the teaching practice. They repeatedly begin with a meaningful situation, give students space to make decisions, make their reasoning observable through categories, routes, code, outputs, or comparisons, and bring the work back to an explanation or general principle. This movement can be expressed as Kick off with a problem or question, Navigate by making assumptions and trying a representation, Observe patterns, results, trade-offs, and limits, then Tie back to the underlying concept and its wider use. KNOT is a later synthesis of these recurring design choices.

The activity sheets document the intended learning process, while the survey provides a quantitative indication of students’ perceived project learning. As reported in Section 9.4, 120 of 124 respondents agreed or strongly agreed that the project helped them see how algorithms relate to real-world systems. Considered alongside the concept ratings, project evaluations, and course scores, this result shows how the course design was paired with evidence about students’ learning. A theoretical reading of the course design

These modes provide different routes into inquiry, and the theories help explain their roles. Dewey's account of reflective inquiry helps interpret the use of a felt problem, experience, evidence, and reflection. Barrows' work on PBL grounds problem-first learning and the facilitator's role; the Problem and Iterative Problem modes make that structure most visible, while activity and intuition can prepare or support it. Wing's account of computational thinking helps explain why students were asked to formulate problems, represent them, decompose them, identify patterns, develop algorithms, and consider efficiency. Meyer and Land's work on liminality offers a lens for interpreting uncertainty around troublesome threshold concepts, rather than treating every difficult topic as a threshold or every learner as following the same sequence.

The map records the teaching approaches used; the survey and topic self-ratings describe students' perceptions; the project sheet records what students reported selecting. Read together, these sources show how the designed learning opportunities, students’ experiences, and their reported applications connect to the study’s learning dimensions.

Interpretation

Ratings were generally positive across the survey. The highest item mean was for seeing algorithms as related to real-world systems (Q19, M = 4.59), while the lowest was for seeking additional resources when facing difficulty (Q10, M = 3.90). The uncertainty item (Q22, M = 3.57) captures that many students did experience being stuck at some point; it should be read alongside the reported clarity and transition items rather than treated as a negative course rating. The comparatively lower resource-seeking score points to an opportunity to strengthen explicit help-seeking and resource-selection supports.

These results describe students’ perceptions of the course and its contribution to their learning. They are interpreted alongside concept-understanding ratings, project evaluations, course scores, and students’ qualitative accounts.

The sixteen liminality case studies were also used in the official study. The survey feedback is reported at course level: 112 of 124 students (90.3%) rated case studies positively for supporting reflection (Q9), and 108 of 124 (87.1%) rated them positively for recognising learning transitions (Q24). These responses reflect students’ overall case-study experience and are not separate evaluations of each case title.

Self-reported understanding across 37 topics

The standalone concept-understanding file contains 128 submissions and 37 topic items, with 123 distinct roll IDs. The response options include “Completely Understood,” “Mostly Understood,” “Somewhat Understood,” “Barely Understood,” and “Not Understood at All.” Item-level valid responses range from 122 to 128 because some answers are blank.

Combining “Completely” and “Mostly” gives a concise view of the strongest self-reported understanding categories. Binary Search Tree is highest at 126 of 128 responses (98.4%), followed by 2-3 Tree at 124 of 128 (96.9%) and Bubble Sort at 123 of 128 (96.1%). In the source category “Not Understood at All,” there are 19 responses across 13 topics; Boyer-Moore has the most (3). The highest number of “Barely Understood” responses is also for Boyer-Moore (12).

The self-reported ratings show variation in students’ reported understanding across topics, especially among string-search algorithms. Binary Search Tree and Bubble Sort received strong combined “Completely” and “Mostly” ratings, while Boyer-Moore, KMP, and Rabin-Karp received comparatively lower shares. These patterns connect topic-level confidence to the broader course account of knowledge construction.

What the combined pattern suggests

Across the map, 10 topics were marked Easy, 19 Medium and 8 Hard; 20 topics had a lab listed. The near-identical pooled “Completely” or “Mostly” shares by planned complexity (Easy 84.6%, Medium 84.0%, Hard 84.2%) suggest that the instructor-assigned complexity labels do not separate student self-ratings in this dataset. That is a descriptive observation only. The hardest items as classified by the instructor were not necessarily rated least clearly, and students’ prior knowledge, sequence, exposure and topic-specific demands are not controlled.

Mode-level pooled self-ratings also vary: Activity and Problem 91.3%, Iterative Problem 89.2%, Problem 83.3%, Traditional 81.5%, Intuition 80.0%, Intuition and Application Problems 76.4%, and Intuition and Storytelling 69.8%. The number of concepts per mode ranges from one to thirteen, and each mode is attached to different concepts. These percentages are not a fair comparison of teaching effectiveness. They help identify questions for the next design cycle: what made the sorting activity and iterative tree sequence legible to students, and what additional examples, practice, or feedback might help with asymptotic notation and string-search algorithms?

Course-level survey responses provide a related but broader signal: 97.6% agreed or strongly agreed that activities and explanations clarified concepts (Q1); 91.9% that cases and activities strengthened conceptual foundations (Q3); and 86.3% that PBL and CT were integrated (Q27). These are not ratings of individual teaching modes. The lower resource-seeking result (69.4% at ratings 4 or 5 on Q10) points to a practical improvement: teach students how to select, evaluate and use resources while they are working through difficult concepts.

9.4 Project evaluation

Students reported a range of project choices

The 2025 project-evaluation sheet contains 120 submissions and 115 distinct roll numbers. Five roll numbers appear twice, so the counts and percentages below describe submissions, not deduplicated students. The repeated records are retained, and all percentages use 120 submissions as the denominator. Multi-select percentages can add to more than 100% because a submission can include several choices.

The sheet records project size and structure, design techniques and principles, data structures and algorithms, SDG mappings, and whether students reported presenting efficiency analysis. The counts summarize students’ reported project choices and documentation.

City design as the first stage of project work

Before developing their algorithmic project, students worked in teams to design a new city. The brief placed the city in a 1,500-square-kilometre area surrounded by existing human habitation and asked teams to prepare a proposal for the Government of India. Students were asked to consider the cautionary examples identified in the brief, including Lavasa and Masdar, and to develop a city plan with a layout, population capacity, budget, revenue models, sustainability provisions, and supporting information. They could make and state assumptions about the location, state context, terrain, climate, and natural features such as mountains and lakes.

The assignment was introduced through the instructor-provided white paper Streets: The Living and Life. It turned observations of Mumbai’s streets and everyday movement into questions that students could develop into project needs. The paper considered public transport and long commutes, crowding and connectivity, differences between neighbourhoods, informal settlements, homelessness, affordability, and the character of public spaces. It also invited comparisons with places such as Matheran, where restrictions on vehicles raise questions about mobility, cost, and environmental quality. Students were encouraged to move from a question to a more developed context and need statement, then propose a design response.

The accompanying Phase 1 planning sheet divided early city research among four team roles. The work included topography, climate, natural resources and postal identity; population capacity, industries, banking and language; air quality, vehicle registration, street naming and government offices; and governance, neighbouring cities, and transport infrastructure such as an airport, metro, BRTS or harbour. These responsibilities made the city a set of connected systems that the team had to bring together in one proposal.

Together, these examples show students moving from a contextual need to a representation, then to a choice of algorithm or data structure and an explanation of its role. This sequence can make abstract concepts more concrete and creates occasions to practise decomposition, abstraction, problem solving, and transfer. The project feedback provides a direct link to students’ reported learning: 120 of 124 respondents (96.8%) agreed or strongly agreed that the project helped them see how algorithms relate to real-world systems (Q19), and the same number reported applying class concepts in project work (Q17). Together, these item-level results show that respondents generally perceived the project as relevant to real-world systems and reported applying course concepts. To examine the project artefacts beyond students’ checkbox and count responses, a retrospective 50-mark evidence rubric was applied through an automated first-pass screen of the latest submission for each roll number. 9.6 Quantitative summary

What the numerical data show

  • Across the 28 survey items, the highest mean was for relating algorithms to real-world systems (Q19, 4.59); resource seeking when facing difficulty was lower (Q10, 3.90). The uncertainty item (Q22) showed the widest variation (SD = 1.04) and is not a course-satisfaction measure.
  • The liminality items describe different aspects of uncertainty and transition; interpret their responses individually rather than as a single scale.
  • The CIE records show a mean theory total of 37.45/50 and a mean lab total of 67.75/80. For 128 students with both records, the combined mean was 105.49/130. These are course performance records, not pre/post evidence of change.
  • Project submissions report frequent use of arrays, queues, heaps, greedy techniques, and graph algorithms. These selections describe reported use, not implementation quality.
  • Self-reported understanding is high for several tree and sorting topics, while string-search topics have the lowest combined “Completely” and “Mostly” shares in the 37-topic file.
  • The project response file includes five repeated roll numbers, and four project identifiers need review before linking project records to CIE scores. Pilot portfolio scores remain excluded from final-study quantitative results.

This chapter presents the quantitative record. Open-ended responses, reflections, and students’ explanations will be presented and analysed in the next chapter.
