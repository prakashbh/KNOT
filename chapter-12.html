12.1 · The path to a framework

KNOT emerged through successive questions

The earlier chapters record a progression rather than a single moment of invention. From 2022 to 2025, the work moved through three research stages, each with a different student batch: initial classroom exploration and feedback in Chapter 5, the pilot study in Chapter 6, and the official study documented in Chapters 8–11. The pilot tested ideas while the questions and survey items were still being refined, and it helped shape KNOT Framework Version 0 in Chapter 7. The official study followed with a clearer research design for examining how knowledge construction was supported and evidenced within the integrated Problem-Based Learning (PBL) and Computational Thinking (CT) course.

Chapters 9 and 10 bring together the quantitative course and survey records, the course-delivery map, final-study case and lab materials, project portfolios, and students’ written accounts. Chapter 11 interprets these sources against the research questions. This chapter draws those strands together to explain what KNOT means in light of the learning opportunities and students’ experiences.

Working meaning. KNOT is a recursive design framework in which meaningful problems invite learners to construct, test, explain, and revise understanding. PBL provides a reason to inquire; CT offers practices for working through problems; and attention to liminality helps a teacher recognize that uncertainty can accompany changing understanding.

12.2 · What the study contributes

Different evidence sources illuminate different parts

No single measure tells the whole story. Course and task materials describe the opportunities designed for students; records describe performance; survey ratings describe perceptions; and open responses show how students explained their experiences. Read together, these sources show how the course supported self-directed learning, problem solving, transfer of knowledge, and the construction of connected understanding.

12.3 · The framework taking shape

KNOT holds the elements in relationship

KNOT stands for Knowledge construction, Not a One-time Transformation. Knowledge construction sits at the centre. The surrounding elements describe conditions and practices that can help students make and remake meaning. They are connected parts of a framework, not a checklist or a guaranteed sequence.

The 2025 teaching materials help make this design logic concrete. The Hungry Loop Cafe case turns repeated price questions and system slowdown into a reason to consider data organization and efficiency. The Invitation Problem asks learners to model places, routes, costs, and constraints before generalizing a solution. Lab 02 moves from everyday grouping and shared-expense problems to programming and comparing alternative solutions. Together with student feedback about applying graph theory to city roads, these materials show why KNOT needs both a meaningful problem and visible opportunities to reason, inspect, explain, and connect ideas.

PBL gives inquiry a purpose

A real or realistic problem provides context, invites questions, and gives students a reason to use ideas.

CT makes reasoning visible

Decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, algorithmic thinking, and checking help learners work with complexity and make their reasoning discussable.

Liminality helps interpret uncertainty

Difficulty, partial understanding, and shifts in explanation may be part of learning. Students need not pass through identical or fixed stages.

Support keeps inquiry productive

Prompts, examples, peer and teacher dialogue, resources, practice, and feedback help students continue without removing the need to think.

12.4 · Designing for the cycle

What a teacher can do with KNOT

KNOT is intended to guide design decisions, not prescribe one correct class format. The study suggests several questions that can help a teacher plan and reflect:

  1. What meaningful problem will give students a reason to engage? Make the context understandable and leave room for students to ask questions.
  2. What thinking should become visible? Ask students to represent the problem, explain a choice, trace a process, compare approaches, or justify efficiency.
  3. Where might learners become uncertain? Anticipate likely sticking points and provide ways to seek help, test a small example, revisit a resource, or work with a peer.
  4. How will students check and communicate an idea? Include opportunities to test, receive feedback, revise, and explain why a solution works.
  5. Where can the idea travel next? Invite application to a changed context and notice when a new problem requires learners to revisit what they thought they knew.
Reading learning transitions. Liminality helps a teacher attend to unsettled or changing understanding. It should not be used to label a student as being in a fixed stage, nor should struggle be treated as proof that learning has occurred. Evidence of the learner’s explanation, decisions, and revision still matters.

Self-directed learning, problem solving, and transfer of knowledge are central capacities in this design. The survey, student accounts, project evaluations, concept ratings, and course scores provide evidence of how these capacities were experienced and demonstrated in the course. Together, they show how these learning dimensions contributed to the emergence of KNOT’s account of knowledge construction.

12.5 · Completing the loop

What the study can say about the main question

The main research question asks how knowledge construction was supported and evidenced within a PBL–CT integrated course for computer science students. Across the evidence, course materials show opportunities to work with meaningful problems, reason with course concepts, practise and check solutions, and connect ideas. Project-feedback items and open responses show that students reported using course concepts in projects and relating algorithms to real systems. Course records add assessed performance; survey and open responses add students’ perceptions and accounts.

Together, these findings suggest that KNOT is a useful way to organize and interpret the design relationships in this course. PBL creates a purposeful setting for inquiry; CT practices give students ways to work on problems and articulate reasoning; responsive support helps them continue through uncertainty; and reflection, checking, explanation, and application make developing understanding available for examination.

The strongest conclusion is about the framework’s design logic and the forms of support students described and encountered. Students did not all follow an identical cycle, and the course elements worked together. The study’s contribution is to show how these connected elements supported students as they made sense of concepts, solved problems, and carried ideas into new contexts.

In answer to the research question: the study indicates that an integrated PBL–CT course can support knowledge construction by giving students purposeful problems and structured ways to reason, test, connect, explain, and revisit ideas, with support available as understanding changes. KNOT brings these relationships together as a recursive framework for designing and reflecting on learning.

12.6 · The final framework

KNOT brings the study’s findings into one account

KNOT places knowledge construction at the centre, with PBL and CT as connected contributions. Liminality offers a way to understand uncertainty and transition. Responsive scaffolding, opportunities to explain and test ideas, and applications across contexts support the work students do as they build and connect understanding.

What this study supports

A coherent account of how the course supported self-directed learning, problem solving, transfer of knowledge, and knowledge construction.

What the evidence shows together

Student feedback, concept ratings, project work, course scores, and designed learning activities describe complementary parts of the same learning experience.

What teachers can do

Use KNOT to plan meaningful problems, support students’ reasoning, observe their thinking, and help them connect learning to new contexts.

The final KNOT framework is the outcome of this research journey. It brings the study’s central insight into a form teachers can carry into planning, teaching, assessment, and reflection.
