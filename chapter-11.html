11.1 The story and the evidence

The questions led to a course, the course led to student accounts, and the accounts return us to the framework

The research began with a question about how an integrated PBL–CT course could support knowledge construction. The pilot informed KNOT Version 0. The final study then examined students’ reported learning, their self-reported understanding of course topics, their project choices, and their descriptions of difficult or meaningful moments. Chapters 9 and 10 bring those evidence streams together for this discussion.

Full course survey

124 response records from 123 distinct roll IDs, covering 28 rating items and ten open-ended prompts.

Concept understanding

128 submissions across 37 topics. These ratings describe students’ perceived understanding.

Project evaluation

120 submissions describing reported project choices and efficiency-analysis coverage.

Sixteen liminality case studies

The cases, drawn from nature, history, art, and everyday systems, were used in both the pilot and the official study. Their titles and descriptions appear in Chapter 6; official-study feedback is reported in Chapter 9.

The research has three stages, each involving a different student batch: initial exploration, pilot study, and official study. The evidence discussed here belongs to the official-study batch. Within that batch, counts vary because some students did not complete the Google survey, submit a project, or continue after getting a year back, and some records have blank items, repeated submissions, or unavailable course data. Each count describes the records available for its analysis; it does not indicate a different batch within the official study. Together, these sources show course-level patterns across student feedback, self-directed learning, problem solving, transfer, project application, and assessed performance.

11.2 Main research question

How is knowledge construction supported and evidenced within a PBL–CT integrated course for computer science students?

The clearest answer is about what students did while trying to understand. The problems and projects gave concepts a purpose. The case-study and lab materials show how that purpose was designed: students were asked to infer useful data structures from a cafe's repeated queries, model a local itinerary with costs and constraints, and move from familiar group problems into code and solution review. Computational thinking practices gave students ways to examine those problems: break them down, represent information, trace steps, compare approaches, notice constraints and ask about efficiency. Students’ accounts suggest that ideas became more usable when they could explain a method, test it, and connect it to another topic or situation. In the survey, 120 of 124 respondents agreed or strongly agreed that the project helped them see how algorithms relate to real-world systems, and an open response describes using graph theory to plan connected city roads. Course records add assessed performance context: among 128 students with both CIE records, the mean theory-plus-lab total was 105.49 out of 130. Read together, the student reports and assessed work show complementary evidence of how the course supported knowledge construction.

The quantitative findings are consistent with positive perceptions, with variation across concepts and students. The highest survey item mean was 4.59 for seeing algorithms as related to real-world systems. The mean was 4.55 for the item about academic and personal growth through course design. On the topic form, 98.4% reported “Completely” or “Mostly” understanding Binary Search Tree, while the corresponding shares were 67.2% for both KMP and Boyer-Moore. Together with the qualitative accounts and course scores, these patterns show where students reported clarity and how their performance varied across the course.

Answer to the main question. The integrated course supported knowledge construction through contextual problems, structured reasoning practices, and opportunities to represent, explain, test, and connect ideas. Student accounts show how self-directed learning, problem solving, and transfer of knowledge formed part of this process. Survey patterns, concept ratings, project evidence, and course scores provide complementary evidence of how students experienced and used these opportunities.

11.3 Sub-question 1 · Design elements

Which features of the PBL–CT course design created opportunities for self-directed learning, problem solving, and transfer of knowledge?

The study points to design elements that work together to support self-directed learning, problem solving, and transfer of knowledge. Authentic cases and projects connected algorithms to a purpose. Physical and visual activities, small examples and tracing made hidden steps visible. Decomposition, comparison and efficiency questions helped students organise their reasoning. Practice, peer discussion, instructor support and feedback helped students keep working when an idea was unclear. These capacities connect to knowledge construction: students take initiative, build and test a solution, and carry an idea into another context.

The final-study case and lab sheets add concrete evidence of how these design elements were put into tasks. The Hungry Loop Cafe problem asks students to formulate frequent questions and reason about why repeated scans slow down as data grows. The Invitation Problem asks students to plan a realistic route, state assumptions, weigh cost and time, then generalize the approach. Lab 02 moves from classifying familiar activities and settling trip expenses in pairs to programming and comparing three LLM-generated solutions, including their prompts and workflows. These are not simply examples attached after instruction: the prompts make problem framing, representation, assumptions, comparison, and reflection part of the work. The survey gives a student-side connection: 96.8% agreed or strongly agreed that the project helped them relate algorithms to real-world systems (Q19), and open responses describe applying graph ideas to city planning. The documents show the intended learning opportunities; the survey shows students’ reported experience of project application.

The concept delivery map makes the design more concrete. It records seven primary modes across 37 topics: direct explanation, problem-first inquiry, iterative problem extension, activity with a problem, intuition, intuition with application problems, and intuition through storytelling. The accompanying teaching notes give practical examples: sorting paper chits before formal analysis, changing a graph traversal from a stack to a queue, and using an unbalanced search tree to motivate balancing and rotations. This suggests a design sequence that can move from an intuitive or visible encounter to formal representation, comparison and application. The survey, concept ratings, project work, and course performance give complementary evidence for interpreting how students engaged with these opportunities.

Student ratings offer a useful connection to this map. The six topics assigned Activity and Problem had a pooled 91.3% “Completely” or “Mostly” self-rating; six Iterative Problem topics had 89.2%. Ratings across Easy, Medium and Hard classifications were very similar at 84.6%, 84.0% and 84.2%. Read alongside students’ descriptions and the course materials, these patterns help explain how different lesson designs supported engagement with concepts. The modes and complexity levels both contribute to the interpretation.

Project choices provide another link between course content and application: the project sheet includes exact selections for 29 of the 37 course topics. For example, students reported Dijkstra in 107 of 120 project submissions, Heap in 106, BFS in 101, and Merge Sort in 80. These are self-reported selections, not verified evidence that a corresponding class lesson produced the project use. The remaining topics have only broader proxies, such as efficiency-analysis reporting or a backtracking selection. The course survey also supports the overall design story: 97.6% agreed or strongly agreed that activities and explanations clarified concepts, while 91.9% said cases and activities strengthened their conceptual foundation. Neither item evaluates a specific mode.

The survey gives a useful design signal. Students rated the item about course design supporting academic and personal growth at 4.55 out of 5. At the same time, seeking additional resources when facing difficulty had a lower mean of 3.90. This points to a practical improvement: make help-seeking, resource choice and checking the validity of an approach more explicit parts of the design.

Answer to SRQ1. The study identified a coordinated set of course-design features that created opportunities for self-directed learning, problem solving, and transfer: meaningful problems, explicit CT reasoning, visible representations, repeated practice, and responsive support. Together, these features inform the design logic expressed in the final KNOT framework: purposeful beginnings, supported inquiry, visible thinking, and connections beyond the immediate task.

What do students report about conceptual understanding, problem solving, and application, and what complementary patterns appear in concept ratings, project work, and course-performance records?

Students generally reported that the course supported learning, but their responses varied by question and topic. The figures below show both the positive pattern and the areas where support may need attention.

The survey and project records show that the course created opportunities to use ideas in context and that students perceived value in those opportunities. An automated, retrospective first-pass rubric screen adds a preliminary indication of what the public portfolio pages documented. For 111 accessible portfolios, the mean evidence score was 22.88 out of 50. Problem and real-world context averaged 4.24 out of 6, while algorithm reasoning averaged 3.77 out of 10. Efficiency discussion averaged 5.42 out of 10. Individual contribution was least visible, averaging 0.59 out of 6. One student described a shift “from knowing individual concepts to seeing how they all fit together.” Together, these records suggest meaningful contextual application, while the portfolios often made the reasoning and individual ownership harder to verify. The rubric is a structured analysis of visible documentation, not the original course grade.

The portfolio review describes the accessible student work through a common set of criteria. Read alongside project feedback, survey responses, and course performance, it shows how students represented algorithms, data structures, and design decisions in larger proposals.

This question asks what evidence indicates support for these outcomes. The survey, concept self-ratings, project records, CIE scores, and the designed tasks provide complementary views of students’ reported experience, application, and performance during the course. Across these sources, self-directed learning, problem solving, and transfer of knowledge connect to the broader process of knowledge construction.

Answer to SRQ2. Students reported growth as problem solvers (103/124, 83.1%), connecting ideas and learning independently (114/124, 91.9%), and academic and personal growth through course design (120/124, 96.8%). Project responses, concept self-ratings, and course scores add complementary evidence; mean CIE scores were 37.45/50 in theory and 67.75/80 in lab. Together, Chapter 9’s survey and performance findings document student-reported improvement and course performance. These findings informed the learning design expressed in KNOT.

11.5 Sub-question 3 · Student experience

How do students describe moments of uncertainty, effort, and emerging clarity as they work through course concepts and tasks?

The ten open-ended prompts gave students room to describe episodes instead of selecting a rating. Across the six themes in Chapter 10, students described moving from procedures to explanations, using visible representations, developing ways to work when stuck, framing and comparing solutions, connecting concepts, and giving course ideas situated meaning.

Procedure to explanation

Students asked why an algorithm took its form.

Action to mental model

Sorting, drawings and tracing made steps inspectable.

Stuckness to strategy

Practice, small examples, help-seeking and teaching supported progress.

Answer to reasoned choice

Some students described decomposing and comparing before choosing.

Separate topics to connections

Students related algorithms, structures and constraints.

Course idea to situated meaning

Cases and projects connected ideas to everyday and real-world contexts.

These accounts show that confidence and uncertainty can coexist. Students described taking initiative and making connections, while also asking for validation, clearer timing or more conceptual preparation. Some progress was partial. One student could connect a concept to a real-world problem but was still unsure which algorithm was effective. The discussion should preserve these accounts instead of presenting only successful moments.

Answer to SRQ3. Students described varied, non-linear experiences involving uncertainty, effort, support, explanation, and provisional clarity. Their accounts show different routes through difficulty, practice, and emerging understanding as they worked through course concepts and tasks.

11.6 Sub-question 4 · Liminality

How can these accounts of uncertainty and emerging clarity be interpreted through liminality, while recognising that students’ learning paths may differ?

Liminality is interpreted here through related but distinct sources. Final-study survey responses show how students described uncertainty and transition: Q9 asked whether the course case studies encouraged reflection (M = 4.39), Q22 asked whether students felt uncertain or stuck (M = 3.57), Q23 asked about later clarity or breakthroughs (M = 3.96), Q24 asked whether case studies helped them recognise transitions (M = 4.20), and Q25 asked whether they could identify movement from confusion to understanding (M = 4.35). Open response 26 invited students to describe a transition and what helped them cross it. These are retrospective accounts, so they indicate perceived experience rather than a measured sequence of change.

What the sixteen cases contribute to this interpretation

Second, the sixteen existing case descriptions help examine the kinds of intellectual movement the activities invite. Students are asked to move from observing phenomena such as repeated patterns, forts, artworks, animal behaviour, or slime mould routes towards algorithmic ideas such as pattern recognition, abstraction, decomposition, transfer, and shortest paths. This movement from a familiar or visible situation to a more abstract computational representation can create a threshold for learning. The cases therefore help us interpret where liminal work may arise in the design.

The sixteen case studies were present in both the pilot and the official study. In the official-study survey, 112 of 124 students (90.3%) agreed or strongly agreed that case studies supported reflection (Q9), and 108 of 124 (87.1%) agreed that they helped students recognise learning transitions (Q24). A further 116 of 124 (93.5%) reported identifying movement from confusion to understanding (Q25). These whole-course responses are not linked to individual case titles, so they show students’ overall perceptions of case-study reflection and transition rather than ratings of each of the sixteen cases separately. Read alongside open response 26 and students’ reflections, the cases help interpret how familiar phenomena could invite movement toward more abstract computational ideas.

Answer to SRQ4. Liminality is useful for interpreting reported uncertainty, effort, emerging explanation and remaining questions. The current evidence supports it as a sensitising lens, not as a validated scale or a sequence that every student crossed.

11.7 What the findings add to KNOT

Keep the recursive idea, and make the support around it more visible

KNOT Version 0 was a working proposition that emerged after the pilot. The final study gives grounds to retain its central relationship: meaningful problems create a context for inquiry, CT practices organise reasoning, and students’ changing understanding can be considered through a liminality lens. The final-study case and lab materials now make the design practice more concrete: students meet a problem, make modelling decisions, inspect efficiency or solution differences, and explain what they learned. Project feedback, including students’ accounts of using graph theory in the city design, shows that some students perceived and described such connections in their work. The student accounts add detail to what knowledge construction looked like in practice.

The strongest design implication is to make validation and method selection explicit. The lab sheets ask students to compare algorithmic and LLM-generated approaches, while survey responses show that some students still wanted confirmation that their reasoning was valid or were unsure which algorithm was effective. The final KNOT framework represents feedback and checking as part of the learning environment, not as a final step after the student has already reached an answer.

The evidence therefore closes the loop: the questions shaped the course and data collection; students’ accounts and ratings reveal promising forms of support and points of difficulty; those findings come together in the final KNOT framework.

11.8 Discussion synthesis

The four learning dimensions form one connected account

The central research question is answered through the convergence of the study’s evidence. The course created problem-centred opportunities; computational thinking gave students ways to represent, decompose, compare, and test ideas; students described taking initiative, solving problems, and transferring learning; and course records documented their work across theory, lab, and project settings.

Self-directed learning, problem solving, and transfer of knowledge are not separate destinations. They describe how learners participate in constructing knowledge. This connection is the main finding carried forward into the final KNOT framework: teachers design meaningful starting points, support students as they reason, observe their thinking, and help them connect what they learn.

Discussion conclusion. Across the research questions, the study shows how an integrated PBL–CT course can support knowledge construction through purposeful problems, visible reasoning, practice, feedback, and application.
