3.1 Contextual Review of Existing Approaches and Their Gaps

Contextual Review of Existing Approaches and Their Gaps

The landscape of computer science education has been shaped through a progression of instructional traditions that include structured teaching, inquiry-oriented environments, and computationally grounded reasoning practices. Each approach contributes valuable strengths, yet significant gaps emerge when these approaches are examined through the expectations placed on learners in contemporary engineering settings. Students today encounter complex problems, shifting contexts, and ambiguous situations. They must analyse, interpret, reorganise, and apply knowledge in ways that extend beyond the boundaries of routine exercises. Understanding how earlier approaches support or limit such learning is therefore essential for identifying which constructs deserve focused attention in the present study.

Traditional instruction places emphasis on clarity during the delivery of concepts. Structured explanations, guided demonstrations, and worked examples give learners confidence with foundational ideas. Research confirms that this approach is effective for memorisation and the application of procedures in familiar settings (Mayer, 2004). Many studies also point out clear limitations. Learners often retain information in forms that align with classroom examples and struggle when asked to approach unfamiliar contexts or reorganise their thinking in meaningful ways (Bransford et al., 2000). These patterns suggest that while structured teaching provides stability, it offers limited support for the deeper cognitive processes through which learners refine and extend their understanding.

Inquiry-oriented designs such as problem-based learning present a contrasting experience. Learners investigate authentic situations, identify knowledge gaps, question prior interpretations, and construct explanations through discussion and reflection. These environments promote sustained engagement and encourage learners to interact with content in ways that challenge them to form their own interpretations (Hmelo-Silver, 2004). Research describes cycles of exploration, articulation, evaluation, and reinterpretation that characterise such settings. At the same time, the degree to which learners regulate their own inquiry varies significantly. Some studies report that students develop strong habits of planning and monitoring their learning, while others indicate reliance on facilitation or group dynamics (Dolmans & Schmidt, 1994). Evidence also shows variation in the extent to which ideas developed in inquiry settings are applied beyond the immediate problem. These variations indicate that inquiry environments promote rich engagement yet do not consistently support the internal processes through which learners reorganise knowledge.

Computationally structured approaches introduce another dimension. Practices such as abstraction, decomposition, pattern identification, and algorithmic design provide orderly methods for analysing problems. These practices help learners organise information and navigate complexity with clarity. Research reports that such approaches strengthen analytical reasoning and cultivate systematic thinking (Wing, 2006; Grover & Pea, 2018). Several studies also highlight limitations. Learners sometimes follow structured procedures without examining how new ideas relate to prior knowledge, and without reflecting on the conceptual implications of their solutions (Weintrop et al., 2016). This indicates that computational thinking supports discipline in reasoning while giving limited attention to the reflective and integrative aspects of learning.

Recent attempts to combine exploratory inquiry with computational structure seek to integrate the strengths of both traditions. These approaches aim to situate learners in authentic situations while providing them with cognitive tools for systematic reasoning. Reports describe increased engagement, clearer analysis, and improved willingness to explore ideas in such blended environments (Kwon et al., 2021). Even so, these early efforts reveal important gaps. Many integrated designs concentrate on structured outcomes and observable performance during tasks. Fewer studies investigate how learners regulate their thinking through uncertainty, how they refine their reasoning as they encounter diverse contexts, or how their ideas become reorganised into coherent understanding.

These observations show that each tradition supports certain dimensions of learning while leaving other areas under-examined. Traditional teaching provides conceptual clarity yet offers little support for deeper reorganisation of ideas. Inquiry environments promote exploration and reasoning but produce variable outcomes in learning regulation and extended application. Computational approaches strengthen systematic analysis yet offer limited opportunities for reflective integration. Integrated designs offer promising progress, although the internal processes through which learners transform their understanding require further investigation.

These gaps create a compelling need for a systematic examination of which learning constructs deserve central focus in an integrated framework. The next section builds on these observations by identifying the central learning outcome and the learning dimensions examined in relation to it.

3.2 Identifying the Central Learning Outcome

Identifying the Central Learning Outcome

A careful examination of existing instructional traditions reveals that several learning outcomes appear consistently in the literature, yet each receives uneven attention across different approaches. Some outcomes are supported clearly in isolated contexts, while others remain conceptually underdeveloped, especially in integrated designs that combine problem-based inquiry and computational reasoning. The purpose of this gap analysis is to compare potential outcome constructs and determine which one offers the greatest conceptual clarity and the strongest justification for focused investigation.

Conceptual understanding appears frequently across studies of traditional and inquiry-oriented teaching. Traditional instruction offers clarity through explanation and structured demonstration, while inquiry settings create opportunities for exploration and discussion. Although this outcome is important, several works indicate that understanding often remains tied to familiar tasks and does not always develop into deeper reorganisation of ideas. This limitation suggests that conceptual understanding alone may not capture the transformation expected in environments that require sustained reasoning.

Task performance is common in studies that emphasise procedural learning and computational techniques. Learners show proficiency by completing tasks correctly or implementing prescribed solution steps. These measures provide useful information about accuracy and fluency, yet they reveal little about how learners reinterpret or refine their internal models of knowledge. Performance can improve without significant conceptual change or reflective activity, which reduces its suitability as a central outcome for a study that examines deeper learning processes.

Engagement with problems is another recurring construct. Learners in exploratory and computational settings frequently show active participation when working with authentic tasks. Engagement supports involvement and persistence, although several studies argue that engagement alone does not explain how learners reorganise ideas or move from surface activity to deeper internal change. Interest and participation can exist without meaningful reconstruction of understanding.

Learning regulation appears in research that examines how learners manage uncertainty, plan their inquiry, and monitor their progress. These behaviours influence the way students respond to complex situations. Studies show variation in the extent to which learners regulate their thinking across instructional designs. Regulation plays an important role in learning, although it functions more as a process that supports deeper transformation rather than as the final outcome of an instructional approach.

Knowledge construction appears in several traditions as a central but often implicit idea. Inquiry environments encourage learners to connect new information with existing knowledge, evaluate interpretations, and refine their understanding through cycles of reflection. Computationally structured methods help learners organise ideas and recognise patterns that support refinement of internal models. Scholarship on meaningful learning emphasises that transformation occurs when learners reorganise their thinking, revise assumptions, and integrate insights across contexts. Studies rarely examine this process within integrated designs that combine inquiry and computational reasoning. This gap suggests that knowledge construction represents a form of learning that carries both conceptual depth and practical relevance for an integrated instructional framework.

The construct appears across multiple traditions, yet it remains under-examined in integrated instructional designs. This combination of conceptual depth and limited prior focus provides a clear rationale for selecting knowledge construction as the central outcome of the study.

3.3 Learning Dimensions Related to Knowledge Construction

Learning Dimensions Related to Knowledge Construction

Once knowledge construction is identified as the central learning outcome, the next step is to clarify which learning dimensions the study will examine in relation to it. In an integrated problem-based and computationally structured environment, learners engage with complexity, reflect on unfamiliar situations, adapt ideas to varied contexts, and regulate their thinking during uncertainty. Several processes appear across instructional traditions, but this study focuses on problem solving, self-directed learning, and transfer of knowledge as learning dimensions that help describe students’ engagement with these demands. The analysis treats them as learning dimensions, not as tested causal pathways.

The literature on inquiry-oriented learning describes several behaviours that shape the reorganisation of ideas. Learners identify gaps in understanding, seek relevant information, revise interpretations when new evidence appears, and coordinate efforts within groups. These behaviours contribute to deeper comprehension, although the degree of engagement with them varies across designs. This variability shows that some processes are closely dependent on the design of the task and facilitation, while others rely more on learner initiative and strategic effort.

Computationally structured approaches introduce systematic practices that influence cognitive activity in distinct ways. Learners work through steps such as abstraction and decomposition, examine relationships among components, and generate potential solution pathways. These practices encourage disciplined thinking and shape how students organise and process complex information. These strategies support structured analysis, although procedural success does not guarantee conceptual reorganisation. Some learners complete computational tasks effectively without examining how their internal understanding shifts throughout the process. This distinction highlights the gap between structured reasoning and deeper conceptual change.

Motivational factors, including interest, persistence, and willingness to participate, appear frequently in learning research. These factors influence the amount of effort learners invest and shape their initial engagement with tasks. They provide important context for learning activity, although they rarely mediate deeper conceptual development. Motivation supports involvement, yet it does not explain how learners restructure knowledge or connect ideas across contexts.

Collaboration also appears in many studies that examine learning in complex environments. Group work introduces opportunities for idea exchange, shared reasoning, and collective interpretation. These interactions enrich the learning environment by helping learners hear alternative viewpoints and test their ideas. These benefits rely heavily on group structure, facilitation, and the nature of the problem. Collaboration enhances the context of learning but does not function as a direct mechanism that drives conceptual reorganisation.

Reflection stands out as a process that contributes directly to the development of knowledge construction. Learners step back from their activity, examine the reasoning they used, evaluate the effectiveness of their strategies, and consider alternative explanations. Reflection strengthens understanding because it encourages learners to examine the structure of their thinking. It serves as the internal space in which interpretation, adjustment, and refinement take place. When reflection is integrated meaningfully, learners are more likely to revise misconceptions, adopt new perspectives, and connect insights across experiences.

Cognitive flexibility is another process relevant to the transformation of understanding. Learners demonstrate flexibility when they shift between viewpoints, reframe problems, and adapt their strategies when confronted with new information. This process encourages the reorganisation of ideas because learners must integrate diverse situational cues, compare interpretations, and adjust conceptual models in response to change. Cognitive flexibility becomes especially important in integrated learning environments where students encounter ambiguity and must reorganise their thinking.

Among these processes, several have direct relevance to knowledge construction. The first is problem solving. This process requires learners to analyse situations, interpret information, evaluate alternatives, and justify decisions while working through complexity. These activities create opportunities for idea refinement and provide the cognitive movement necessary for conceptual development.

The second process is self-directed learning. This involves learners identifying what they understand, recognising what remains unclear, and planning actions to address the gap. It includes selecting resources, monitoring progress, and adjusting strategies in response to difficulty. These behaviours support the sustained cognitive effort required when learners confront uncertainty and work with demanding tasks. They influence the extent to which learners are able to reorganise their thinking.

The third process is transfer of knowledge. Learners attempt to apply ideas in contexts that differ from the settings in which the ideas were first introduced. These attempts reveal the flexibility of understanding and require learners to adapt, refine, and restructure conceptual models. Transfer of knowledge is a learning dimension in this study. Applying ideas in a different context can show how learners examine relationships, recognise patterns, and adapt their understanding to new demands.

The comparison shows that problem solving, self-directed learning, and transfer of knowledge have the strongest conceptual links to knowledge construction. Each process contributes to the movement and refinement of ideas in distinct ways. Accordingly, the present study focuses on three learning dimensions: problem solving, self-directed learning, and transfer of knowledge. They are examined in relation to knowledge construction, but the study does not test a statistical mediation model.

3.4 Conclusion

Conclusion

The comparison of learning processes shows that several mechanisms influence how students develop conceptual depth when they work in complex environments. Reflection and cognitive flexibility contribute to learning in meaningful ways, yet they depend strongly on task conditions and often emerge as secondary effects of broader processes. Motivation, collaboration, and structured reasoning enrich the learning context, although they do not consistently guide the internal movement that leads to conceptual reorganisation.

Three learning dimensions stand out as the focus of this study: problem solving, self-directed learning, and transfer of knowledge. The study examines how they relate to knowledge construction within an integrated course. Problem solving directs analytical activity and encourages learners to test, refine, and adjust their ideas. Self-directed learning supports the regulation of thought through monitoring, planning, and revision during uncertainty. Transfer of knowledge promotes flexible reorganisation of ideas when learners attempt to apply their understanding in varied contexts. These three processes appear consistently across traditions and have strong conceptual links to the transformation that knowledge construction represents.

The selection of these learning dimensions completes the gap analysis and establishes the foundation for the study. The next stage organises the instructional design, the three learning dimensions, and knowledge construction into an integrated conceptual framework.
