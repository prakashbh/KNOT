A note from the researcher

Foreword

This research grew from a question I carried into the classroom: how can I make the next class more curious, more active, and more meaningful for my students?

A classroom question becomes a research journey

This work was carried out as a research exploration, and it has led to several deliverables along the way. The starting point, though, was the classroom. As a teacher, I have always looked forward to the next class with excitement about what new thing I could try. Across more than fifteen years of teaching, each class has offered an eye-opening moment: a new way to ask a question, invite curiosity, or help students become more inquisitive. I kept returning to the same questions: how can I make my class more curious? How can I encourage students to investigate, rather than simply receive, what I teach?

The research did not begin in a single day. Its most concentrated stretch began around 2020, when I completed the Problem-Based Learning certification One Day One Problem from Singapore Polytechnic. That experience excited me and gave shape to ideas I had been exploring in my teaching. Later that year, I published a paper on One Day Many Problems, an approach that I felt fitted the Indian context. These experiences became an important starting point for the work that followed.

Since then, the exploration has continued through reading, experimentation, and further learning. A course on Problem-Based Learning from Aalborg helped me understand the philosophy more deeply and appreciate its scope. I also conducted model-thinking workshops where we tried out different aspects of Problem-Based Learning and Computational Thinking. Each workshop brought new questions and helped me examine how these ideas might work in practice.

This work has been shaped through collaboration and calibration: trying an idea, observing what happened, reflecting on it, and adjusting the next attempt. Undergraduate students participating in Research Experience for Undergraduates contributed to some small experimental activities. The papers, workshops, and experiments have all been part of one continuing effort to understand how learning can be made more active and thoughtful.

A note on the use of AI

The research questions, overall direction, classroom work, source data, and most of the research methods originated with me. My research-design documents and study materials set out what evidence to collect and how it would address the questions. I used AI as a supporting tool to help organise and analyse the quantitative data, apply and refine the qualitative coding approach and themes, develop and apply a retrospective rubric to portfolio evidence, and draft and edit parts of this digital manuscript. AI helped with the analysis and presentation of the work; it did not originate the research questions, overall design, planned data sources, or source data. I checked the outputs against the source material and made the final decisions about what to include and how to interpret the findings. AI also assisted with grammar, sentence refinement, and the code used to create this website. I take responsibility for the final content and conclusions.
