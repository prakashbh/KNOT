15.1 Answering the central question

Knowledge construction grew through connected learning experiences

Research question

How is knowledge construction supported and evidenced within a PBL–CT integrated course for computer science students?

The conclusion rests on the official-study evidence reported in Chapter 9, interpreted alongside the qualitative accounts in Chapter 10 and brought together against the research questions in Chapter 11. Chapter 9 shows how the course was designed and what students reported and demonstrated through survey responses, concept ratings, project records, portfolios, and theory and lab CIE results. Chapter 10 adds students’ accounts of how their thinking developed. Earlier chapters establish the educational background, course context, initial exploration, pilot, and research design that led to this official study.

Within the official course, meaningful problems and structured thinking practices gave students ways to reason through tasks. Students encountered contexts, made representations, decomposed problems, compared approaches, tested ideas, explained choices, and connected learning to other contexts. Practice, dialogue, feedback, and reflection were part of the conditions in which students described developing their understanding. In Chapter 9, 103 of 124 respondents (83.1%) reported growing as problem solvers, 114 (91.9%) reported connecting ideas, reflecting, and learning independently, and 120 (96.8%) agreed that the course design supported academic and personal growth. These reports are complemented by project and CIE evidence.

The three learning dimensions identified in the research design describe how students took part in this process. Self-directed learning involved taking initiative, choosing resources, and monitoring progress. Problem solving involved representing a problem, reasoning through constraints, selecting strategies, and checking decisions. Transfer of knowledge involved connecting course ideas to projects, other concepts, and real situations. Together, these dimensions help explain the wider process of knowledge construction, as reflected in students’ accounts and the available course evidence.

15.2 Returning to the research questions

Each sub-question adds a part of the answer

SRQ1 · Design features

Which features created opportunities for the three learning dimensions?

Meaningful problems, explicit computational-thinking practices, visible representations, iterative practice, and responsive support gave students ways to direct their learning, solve problems, and connect ideas to applications. The materials show the opportunities designed into the course; student feedback and accounts show how they experienced some of them.

SRQ2 · Student reports and course evidence

What did students report, and what complementary patterns appeared?

Chapter 9 shows perceived growth: 103/124 respondents (83.1%) reported growing as problem solvers; 114/124 (91.9%) reported connecting ideas, reflecting, and learning independently; 120/124 (96.8%) said course design supported academic and personal growth. Concept self-ratings, project records, and assessed performance add complementary evidence. Theory CIE averaged 37.45/50 (n=129) and lab CIE averaged 67.75/80 (n=128).

SRQ3 · Student experience

How did students describe uncertainty, effort, and clarity?

Across the qualitative themes, students described practising, seeking help, tracing and testing ideas, connecting concepts, and reaching explanations that made sense to them. Their accounts also include continuing uncertainty and requests for clearer support, so the learning paths are not presented as uniform.

SRQ4 · Liminality

How can these experiences be read through liminality?

Liminality helps interpret accounts of uncertainty, partial understanding, effort, and emerging clarity. It is a lens for reading possible transitions, not a measured scale or a fixed sequence every student passed through.

The answers connect back to the central question: knowledge construction was supported through purposeful problems, ways to reason and make ideas visible, and opportunities to test, explain, and carry understanding forward. In the official-study survey, 120 of 124 respondents agreed or strongly agreed that the project helped them see how algorithms relate to real-world systems, and 118 of 124 said the structure of projects and assignments supported deep learning. Student accounts described applying graph theory to city-road planning and connecting concepts. Project evaluations and portfolios add evidence of how students represented algorithms and data structures in larger proposals. Together, Chapter 9’s quantitative findings, Chapter 10’s student accounts, and Chapter 11’s integrated discussion show how the final KNOT framework emerged from the study.

15.3 Contribution of the study

The insight is in how the learning dimensions connect

The main insight is that self-directed learning, problem solving, and transfer are not separate outcomes added beside knowledge construction. They are ways students participate in constructing knowledge. Taking initiative brings the learner into the work. Problem solving gives the learner a way to organise and test ideas. Transfer connects an emerging understanding to a new problem, project, or context. This connection is the study’s central contribution to thinking about integrated PBL and CT in computer science education.

The study also shows the value of designing for visible thinking. When students explain a choice, trace a process, compare an approach, or connect an algorithm to a larger purpose, teachers can respond to the thinking taking place. This gives assessment a role within learning: it helps teachers and students notice what is understood and what deserves another look.

The philosophy can be remembered as Tied, Tried, Retied: learning is tied to a problem, tried through thinking, and retied when understanding travels into what comes next. The final framework translates this insight into teacher decisions, with an outward connection from application to a new context or question.

15.4 The final KNOT framework

A practical form for the study’s learning philosophy

KNOT means Knowledge construction, Not a One-time Transformation. The final framework expresses the study’s conclusion as four teacher-facing moves: Kick off, Navigate, Observe, and Tie back. It is the finished framework developed through this research.

Kick offBegin with a meaningful problem that gives learning a purpose.
NavigateHelp students represent, question, decompose, and work through the problem.
ObserveNotice student thinking through explanations, attempts, decisions, and assessment.
Tie backConnect the learning to concepts, applications, and questions that can travel beyond the class.

The cycle is simple enough to use in a lesson plan and flexible enough for a short activity, a lab, or a larger project. Each move keeps the teacher attentive to what students are thinking and to how ideas are being connected.

15.5 Limitations

How the evidence should be understood

The research journey ran from 2022 to 2025 through three stages, each with its own student batch: initial exploration, pilot study, and official study. The stages inform the development of KNOT but are not a single longitudinal cohort. Within each stage, source counts vary because not every student completed every survey or submitted every project, and some records are blank, repeated, or unavailable. The conclusions are grounded in the course, its teaching materials, available student records, and the teaching context in which KNOT developed.

  • The survey and concept-understanding form report students’ perceptions and self-ratings. These are valuable accounts of experience, while remaining different from independently administered tests of conceptual understanding. The concept file contains 128 submissions from 123 distinct roll IDs, with five IDs appearing twice; the chapter reports the submitted records.
  • The four core survey domains have modest internal-consistency estimates: Cronbach’s alpha ranges from .595 to .700, and McDonald’s omega ranges from .636 to .708. The domain summaries are interpreted with these estimates in mind. Qualitative themes are the researcher’s framework-method interpretation; no second-coder agreement statistic was calculated.
  • The portfolio rubric was applied retrospectively as an automated first-pass analytic screen using page text and link metadata. It was not the original course grading instrument; pages were not fully visually reviewed, code was not executed, and four portfolio records could not be scored because the pages or links were unavailable. The scores therefore describe visible documentation in accessible portfolios, not a comprehensive judgement of student work or ability.
  • The pilot findings were exploratory. Teaching modes were combined across the course, paired case-study tasks differed, and the interview summaries were not produced through a documented qualitative coding procedure. The project-indicator factor analyses used three indicators, and the reported confirmatory analysis did not include model-fit indices. These pilot findings informed the later research design.
  • The initial exploration file does not state how many students were invited to respond, so a response rate cannot be calculated. Some project profile categories also leave band boundaries unspecified, including the values around 500 and 3,001 lines of code, 10 and 31 functions, and 16 files or figures.
  • Survey responses and course performance provide evidence of student-reported growth and learning in the official study. Because the stages involve different batches and the official study does not isolate each KNOT move in a comparison design, the evidence supports the framework as an evidence-informed account of the course, not a causal estimate for each individual framework component.
  • The findings explain how KNOT emerged in this course context; its use in other settings will depend on their learners, subjects, and teaching conditions.

15.6 Future scope

Carry the final framework into other learning settings

The future scope is to put the final KNOT framework to work in new contexts and learn from how teachers adapt it. Further studies can examine its use across other computer science topics, courses, institutions, and disciplines, and document how Kick off, Navigate, Observe, and Tie back appear in different lesson plans and assessments.

Across courses and disciplines

Explore how teachers frame meaningful problems and connect subject-specific reasoning to knowledge construction.

Across learners and settings

Understand how students with different prior experiences use resources, collaborate, and move through uncertainty.

Across time and applications

Examine how students carry concepts into later courses, projects, and professional situations.

In teacher practice

Study how lesson planning and assessment can make student thinking more visible and help teachers respond to it.

This future work can extend the evidence base around KNOT while keeping the framework itself as the final contribution of the present research.

15.7 Closing reflection

A classroom question, carried forward

This research began with the excitement of thinking about what might make the next class more curious. Across exploration, study, analysis, and reflection, that question became a framework for designing learning. KNOT asks teachers to start with purpose, attend to student thinking, and help knowledge connect beyond a single lesson.

A class becomes a place for knowledge construction when students have a reason to think, room to explore, support as they work, and a chance to connect what they learn to something that matters.
