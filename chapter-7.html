01 The proposition

Understanding does not stop at one answer

KNOT is a recursive framework for knowledge construction through the integration of Problem-Based Learning (PBL), Computational Thinking (CT), and liminality. An authentic problem gives learning a context and purpose. CT offers ways to reason through the problem. Liminality helps interpret moments when students’ existing understanding is unsettled and being reconstructed.

Knowledge construction is the central process in the framework. Understanding may become clear enough to explain or apply, but that clarity is provisional. A new problem, context, or contradiction can reveal a limitation and invite another round of inquiry.

KNOT describes a relationship between problems, learner thinking, support, and the construction of meaning. It does not prescribe one fixed teaching sequence.

03 Core components

What each part contributes

The framework keeps its central elements distinct. PBL contributes the learning context; CT contributes reasoning practices; liminality offers a way to interpret transitional experience; scaffolding describes support that can change as students work. Knowledge construction is the process that connects these elements.

These are ways to examine what may happen as understanding changes. They can overlap, repeat, or appear differently across problems; they are not four required lesson stages.

Related capacities to examine

Problem solving · Self-directed learning · Transfer of knowledge

These are important capacities that may develop alongside knowledge construction. The framework does not treat them as guaranteed effects. Their development depends on the learning tasks, the support available, and opportunities to use ideas in new contexts.

04 Recursive movement

A guide to learning, not a lesson script

KNOT does not require every class to move through four fixed steps. A teacher may begin with an explanation, story, case study, experiment, or unfamiliar problem. The design question is whether students have an opportunity to examine their current understanding, notice its limits, work on ideas, and use what they have constructed.

01

Meet a problem

A context or task connects with students’ prior understanding and may reveal a question that is not yet resolved.

02

Work through uncertainty

Students ask questions, identify missing information, compare interpretations, and seek resources. CT practices can help organise this inquiry.

03

Construct and test meaning

Students reconsider assumptions, connect ideas, explain decisions, and test a possible solution. Feedback and reflection can make changes visible.

04

Apply and revisit

Students use their understanding in the task or a new context. A new challenge may strengthen an idea or show where another cycle is needed.

About the liminal positions: Pre-liminal, liminal, and post-liminal describe a learner’s relationship to a particular concept or threshold. They are not permanent categories. “Post-liminal” clarity is provisional and does not mean that learning is complete.

05 What the pilot informs

From the pilot proposition to the final framework

The pilot brought together problem cases, activities, laboratory work, projects, surveys, and student reflections. Across these sources, the study attended to the value of context, how students reasoned through problems, where abstraction remained difficult, and how reflection and application could help connect ideas. These observations informed the first arrangement of PBL, CT, liminality, scaffolding, and knowledge construction in KNOT.

The pilot did not evaluate a completed KNOT framework. This diagram and its components are a conceptual interpretation of the pilot, not evidence that the framework caused improvements in learning. The main study later examined the related course design and student experiences. Chapters 9–11 present and discuss that evidence, while Chapter 12 explains how the final KNOT framework emerged from the study.
