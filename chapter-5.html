5.1 Purpose and setting

A course taking shape through exploration

The work described here took place in the 2022–23 Design and Analysis of Algorithms course with its own student batch, separate from the later pilot and official study. At this stage, I was trying different ways to make the subject more engaging and connected to students’ experience. The course included assignments, a portfolio, case studies, a course project, classroom activities, a search-engine task, a DFS and BFS challenge, a ChatGPT lab, and a techno-fun week. These were teaching experiments within the course, not parts of a validated framework.

At the end of the course, students were invited to provide feedback on their experience. The survey asked them to rate course assignments, the portfolio, case studies, the course project, and course delivery. It also asked which assignment they liked most, whether classroom activities and the techno-fun week helped, and what they would change. The purpose of this chapter is to learn from that feedback and show how it informed the later pilot study.

Initial exploratory research question. How did students experience the DAA course and its learning activities, and what did they consider helpful or in need of improvement?

This open question reflected the exploratory purpose of the work. It helped surface issues for the more focused PBL–CT pilot question presented in Chapter 6.

Attendance was self-reported: 32 respondents selected 100%, 37 selected 80 to 99%, 11 selected 60 to 80%, and one selected less than 60%.

How to read these findings. This exploratory feedback captures the views of the 81 respondents and offers early insight into how students experienced the course and its activities.

5.2 What students reported

Students valued applied and varied course experiences

Ratings were generally favourable. The course project and course delivery received the highest average ratings, followed closely by assignments and case studies. The portfolio was also rated positively, though it received the lowest average among these areas. Ratings use the full 1 to 5 scale, where 1 is highly ineffective and 5 is highly effective. All 81 respondents rated each of these five areas. For example, 75 students rated course assignments as effective or highly effective, while six selected the middle rating or lower. For the portfolio, 62 selected 4 or 5 and 19 selected 3. Portfolio commitment was more varied: 54 students rated their commitment 4 or 5, 23 selected 3, and four selected 2. The portfolio appears to have been well received, but the responses also suggest that its timing, workload, and connection to course concepts deserved closer attention.

The favourite-assignment responses add another view. The course project was selected most often (21), followed by the search-engine task (16) and portfolio (12). ChatGPT Lab was selected by nine respondents. The remaining responses named Code the Philosophy (5), Inventory (5), Dijkstra Lab (5), the DFS-BFS challenge (4), case studies (3), and one other response. Students did not converge on a single activity. This variety supports keeping multiple routes into the subject rather than assuming one activity will engage everyone in the same way.

For classroom activities, 75 respondents selected “Yes” and four selected “Maybe.” Two gave a written qualification instead of a yes or no. Their comments point to participation and interaction as matters to examine, not merely whether activities were present. For techno-fun week, 60 said it helped, 14 described it as fun rather than helpful for learning, and seven gave context such as competing deadlines, illness, or a qualified positive response. The activity seems to have created enjoyment for many, while its learning purpose was not equally clear to everyone.

5.3 What the comments added

Positive feedback sat beside practical requests

Forty of the 81 respondents wrote something in the improvement field. Many expressed appreciation or said that no change was needed. Others gave specific suggestions. Reading both kinds of response matters: the positive ratings show acceptance, while the suggestions show where course design could be made more deliberate.

“I personally feel that more emphasis should be given on hard coding skills on problems of the Algorithms that have been taught in the classes, instead of just translating the algorithm to code...”Anonymous course feedback, 2022–23

Several comments point toward a gap between understanding an explanation and independently producing a solution. Students asked for more coding practice, opportunities to solve a problem themselves, and attention to copying or memorising code. This is not evidence that students lacked problem-solving ability. It is evidence that some students wanted more occasions to demonstrate and strengthen that ability through their own work.

Other comments raised the pace of early classes and the timing of the portfolio. One student suggested placing the portfolio after students had encountered some DSA concepts. This suggests a sequencing question: open-ended exploration can spark curiosity, but learners may benefit from a small base of concepts before they are asked to connect examples to formal algorithms. The survey does not tell us which timing is best, so the question needed to be explored through course design.

Students also valued the course’s relevance and energy while asking for more interaction among classmates. One respondent described activities as useful but noted that participation felt formal and largely teacher-led. This distinction shaped a useful design question for later work: how can an activity move from being something students watch or follow to something they actively discuss, test, and explain?

Finally, some students suggested more real-world motivation, regular coding contests, or a different programming language. These are contextual suggestions rather than direct evidence about the effectiveness of PBL or computational thinking. They nevertheless show that students judged the course through several connected experiences: relevance, coding fluency, pacing, participation, and the language used for implementation.

Analytic reading. The central insight from this feedback is a productive tension. Students appreciated the engaging and applied course, yet some wanted more time and structure to practise independently, interact with each other, and connect exploratory tasks with concepts already taught. The next study needed to examine how those elements could be brought together deliberately.

5.4 What this exploration means

What the exploration contributed

The early feedback pointed toward a study of how students experience active, problem-centred learning and how they connect course ideas to meaningful tasks. Students’ comments brought attention to relevance, variety, practice, peer interaction, pacing, and the place of portfolio work in the semester. These ideas informed the questions and evidence sources taken into the pilot study.

Carry forward as design ideas

Keep relevance, variety, and active work

Students’ responses supported retaining authentic projects, cases, portfolio work, classroom activities, and opportunities to connect algorithms with meaningful problems. The activities should make room for students to solve, explain, and discuss, not only observe a teacher-led demonstration.

Examine more closely

Plan practice, pacing, and sequencing

The comments called for more independent coding practice, peer interaction, careful pacing, and a considered point in the semester for open-ended portfolio work. The pilot could explore these issues through multiple forms of classroom and assessment evidence.

Shape the next inquiry

Ask how learning takes place

The feedback motivated a closer study of how students take initiative, work through problems, and carry concepts into projects and other contexts. These questions became part of the later research design.

5.5 From exploration to pilot

The questions that led to a more structured study

The 2022–23 feedback did not produce the KNOT framework by itself. It helped make the next questions visible. Students appeared to value meaningful contexts and varied activities, while also asking for more opportunities to work through problems independently, practise coding, take part in classroom interaction, and encounter activities at a suitable pace and point in the course.

What the pilot needed to investigate

How could authentic problems and computational thinking be joined with guided inquiry, individual problem-solving practice, reflection, and opportunities to use knowledge in a new context? What evidence would show not just that students enjoyed an activity, but how their understanding developed through it?

These questions led to the pilot study in Chapter 6. The pilot brought together a broader set of evidence, including student surveys, interviews, written assessment, case studies, lab work, and projects. It examined learning experiences more systematically and helped shape the first version of the KNOT framework. The initial exploration reported here remains an important earlier stage: it records how classroom experience and student feedback informed the move from trying activities to studying their role in knowledge construction.
