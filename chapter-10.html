10.1 Scope and method

Reading the open responses as accounts of learning in motion

This chapter analyses the ten open-ended items in the 2025 DAA survey export. They asked students to describe concepts that became clearer, initiative, problem-solving, application beyond class, movement from uncertainty to clarity, helpful and difficult course features, and links between ideas.

The course-delivery map, teaching-mode notes, case-study prompts, and lab sheets are used as context for interpreting these accounts. They show what students were asked to do, including classify familiar activities, plan routes, reason about repeated data queries, program with stacks and queues, and compare proposed solutions. The sixteen liminality case studies were used in the pilot and also in the official study; the official survey and open responses provide the main-study feedback analysed here. Teaching materials provide context and are not coded as student responses.

124survey response records
10open-ended questions
1,237non-empty response entries
122–124responses per item

The name and email columns were excluded from analysis. Only the ten open-ended question fields were counted. Entries ranged from detailed learning episodes to short phrases, general evaluations, and non-responses. Quotations are anonymized excerpts; ellipses mark omissions.

Hybrid Framework Method

Deductive domains came from the research questions and design: self-directed learning, problem-solving, transfer of knowledge, experiences of uncertainty and change, and course conditions students perceived as helping or hindering learning. These sensitised the reading without determining the result. Inductive coding captured students’ own ideas: physical enactment, tracing, second attempts, noticing algorithms in daily life, AI verification, feedback needs and uneven progress.

The analysis proceeded at the response-entry level, with each entry kept alongside its prompt so its meaning was not separated from the question asked. First, the responses were read across all ten prompts. Deductive domains from the research questions and design provided an initial organising frame; inductive codes were added when students described ideas not adequately captured by that frame. Code families were compared across responses, refined to retain differences as well as recurrence, and then synthesized into the six themes below. Quotations are selected verbatim from the anonymized responses to illustrate the interpretation; they are not linked to individual portfolios or course marks.

10.2 Developed themes

Six themes show how students described developing their thinking

The themes reach beyond “activities were helpful.” Together they show how students described developing explanation, representation, learning agency, strategy, connection and confidence.

Theme 1 · Procedure → explanation

Students began asking why an algorithm takes the form it does

Some described learning how a method emerged, what problem it addresses and why it works, rather than treating code as a fixed sequence. Seeing the trial-and-error behind an algorithm made its design intelligible.

Inference: students were acquiring criteria to judge and revise solutions, not only adding procedures to memory.

Theme 2 · Action → mental model

Physical and visual representations made hidden steps available to reason about

Paper sorting, handouts, drawings, small inputs and tracing let students inspect operations that code alone had left opaque. The strongest accounts connected the model back to algorithm steps and implementation.

Inference: learners coordinated representations,physical or drawn model, algorithmic process and code. That offers a more precise account of why some activities stayed memorable.

Theme 3 · Stuckness → learning strategy

Students learned ways to make progress when an idea did not click

They described choosing a small input, tracing repeatedly, returning to notes, comparing explanations, asking a peer or instructor, and teaching the idea. One student estimated spending 1.5–2 hours tracing Towers of Hanoi before later reconstructing it in minutes.

Inference: students were developing a repertoire for constructing knowledge. Explaining to peers shows how students reworked an idea so that they could communicate it to others.

Theme 4 · Answer-seeking → reasoned choice

Problem-solving became framing, decomposing and comparing

Students described pausing before jumping to an answer, splitting a task, identifying constraints, comparing algorithms and asking why a method might fail. Some reported moving from accepting an LLM answer to testing or directing it.

Inference: problem-solving agency means generating and judging a path forward, not merely writing more code. Yet students’ own examples show algorithm selection was not always technically secure.

Theme 5 · Separate topics → connected toolkit

Students built relationships among algorithms, structures and constraints

Some explained how ideas relate, rather than listing them: sorting and searching, divide-and-conquer, priority queues, graph algorithms, data structures and efficiency. Others said comparing multiple solutions helped them select among them.

Inference: this suggests relational knowledge construction: organising concepts by shared structures, relationships and trade-offs instead of storing isolated units. Students’ project accounts and evaluation records also show how they connected course concepts with project decisions.

Theme 6 · Course idea → situated meaning

Cases and projects made concepts matter beyond worked examples

The final-study tasks gave students contexts in which to connect algorithms with routes, services, changing data and business decisions. The city-design brief asked teams to connect planning needs; other cases asked students to reason about a local itinerary or a system slowed by repeated data scans. In the project-feedback response, one student described applying graph theory to plan connected city roads. Some said they began noticing possible algorithmic patterns in daily life. This suggests perceived relevance and an altered way of attending to problems.

Inference: context gave concepts personal meaning and a place to be applied. Student accounts, project evaluations, portfolios, and course scores together show perceived relevance and application of course ideas.

10.3 Knowledge construction synthesis

An emergent cycle: represent, reason, test, connect and revise

Across themes, knowledge construction became visible when students connected a problem representation to an explanation, tried a strategy, and revised their understanding through application or feedback. This interpretation connects recurring student accounts to the course’s integrated design. Students described different paths through this work, often returning to earlier ideas as they learned more.

1 · EncounterMeet a problem or unfamiliar idea→2 · RepresentDraw, sort, model or trace→3 · ExplainAsk why; notice patterns and constraints→4 · ChooseDecompose and compare strategies→5 · ApplyTry the idea in a case or project→6 · Check and reviseUse feedback, practice and reflection

What students brought

Prior intuitions, code habits, partial familiarity, misconceptions and uncertainty about where an algorithm fits.

What they did

Represented, traced, questioned, practised, discussed, searched, compared, tested or tried again.

What they said they built

Explanations, approaches to problems, links across concepts and confidence to try or teach.

What remained in progress

Implementation, method selection, validation, pacing and transfer to a new context.

Central inference: the clearest reported shift is not simply “I know more algorithms,” but “I can make an algorithm intelligible, decide what to try, and relate it to another problem.” This capacity was developing, uneven and not necessarily complete.

Conditions around the cycle: concrete examples, clear explanations, time to practise, peer and faculty support, useful feedback and chances to revisit ideas. Students also described pace, unclear expectations and limited validation as interruptions.

10.4 High-value “aha” moments

The insight was often a change in what students could see

“I can see the logic.”

Physical sorting and tracing changed code into a process students could inspect.

“I can explain why.”

Understanding the problem and rationale gave a procedure purpose.

“I can recover when stuck.”

Small examples, practice, notes, peers and faculty became strategies that learners could combine.

“I can choose, not just copy.”

Decomposition, constraints and comparison offered a way to approach a problem before code or an AI answer.

“These topics belong together.”

Some students began connecting structures, algorithm design, efficiency and application into a broader map.

“I notice ideas outside class.”

Students began interpreting familiar experiences through course concepts, connecting classroom ideas with situations around them.

10.5 Variation and counter-evidence

Progress was partial or unsettled for some students

Some students understood theory but struggled to implement it in code, choose an effective algorithm, connect formal ideas to real situations, or know whether a case-study answer was valid. One wrote they were “still in transition”; some did not recall taking initiative. The accounts therefore describe uneven progress rather than universal transformation.

Students also differed on the timing of open-ended work. One preferred case studies after the related concept had been taught; others said encountering a challenge first motivated exploration. Initiative was not always curiosity-led: one student wrote, “I realised I have to study otherwise I will fail.” That response complicates any simple claim that the course produced intrinsic self-direction.

Reported interruptions included fast pace, limited time, long sessions without breaks, dense coverage, unclear task expectations, activities whose purpose was not obvious, and insufficient feedback. Some asked for revision, extra examples or implementation guidance; others found activities excessive or a task unhelpful. Students’ algorithm examples also sometimes conflate methods. These accounts show students attempting to carry concepts into unfamiliar contexts and raise the importance of explanation and validation during that work.

10.6 Relation to the research design

What these accounts contribute to the three learning dimensions

  • Self-directed learning: initiating practice, choosing resources, asking peers or faculty, exploring applications and checking AI suggestions. Initiative varied, from curiosity and project ownership to exam pressure or reliance on others.
  • Problem-solving: decomposition, pattern recognition, tracing, comparison and attention to constraints or efficiency. Continued difficulty with implementation, selection and validation shows development was uneven.
  • Transfer of knowledge: cases and the city project let students try concepts beyond worked examples. Their accounts connect course ideas with new problems and applications.

Central research question

How is knowledge construction supported and evidenced within a PBL–CT integrated course for computer science students?

These qualitative responses address the “how” in that question by describing processes students associate with learning in the course. Read with the quantitative patterns and performance evidence in Chapter 9, they connect self-directed learning, problem solving, and transfer to the broader process of knowledge construction. Read together, the accounts suggest that PBL gave students meaningful problems to work on, CT offered ways to reason through them, and responsive support helped them persist through uncertainty. Students described constructing or revising explanations and trying ideas in context. These themes connect directly to self-directed learning, problem solving, and transfer of knowledge as capacities through which students built and used understanding. This interpretation fits the recursive KNOT proposition: learners may revisit a concept as a new problem, limit or contradiction appears.

These qualitative findings connect to the central research question and framework. Read alongside Chapter 9’s survey patterns, concept-understanding ratings, project evaluations, portfolios, and course scores, they show a coherent course-level account: students described taking initiative, reasoning through problems, and carrying ideas into applications, while performance records provide an additional view of their work. Together, these sources explain how the integrated course supported knowledge construction.

10.7 Qualitative synthesis

Student accounts connect the four learning dimensions

The themes connect self-directed learning, problem solving, and transfer to the larger work of constructing knowledge. Students described taking initiative, making ideas visible, trying strategies, seeking feedback, and carrying concepts into project and everyday contexts. These themes align with the survey patterns, concept ratings, project evaluations, and course scores presented in Chapter 9.

Read together, the qualitative and quantitative findings provide a coherent account of how students experienced and used the learning opportunities. Variation across students remains part of that account: some described a clear breakthrough, while others described uncertainty or a need for further explanation.

Chapter conclusion: students most clearly described knowledge construction as making an idea visible, finding an explanation, trying a strategy, linking it to another problem and revising understanding. The “aha” was not always getting the answer; often it was discovering how to think when the answer was not yet clear. That shift was meaningful, uneven and dependent on opportunities for practice and feedback.
