8.1 Research questions

The questions guiding the study

The study was guided by a central question and four related questions. Together, they examine course-design opportunities, student reports and course evidence, students’ experiences, and the interpretation of learning transitions.

Research question

How is knowledge construction supported and evidenced within a PBL–CT integrated course for computer science students?

  1. Design features: Which features of the PBL–CT course design created opportunities for self-directed learning, problem solving, and transfer of knowledge?
  2. Student reports and course evidence: What do students report about conceptual understanding, problem solving, and application, and what complementary patterns appear in concept ratings, project work, and course-performance records?
  3. Student experience: How do students describe moments of uncertainty, effort, and emerging clarity as they work through course concepts and tasks?
  4. Liminality interpretation: How can these accounts of uncertainty and emerging clarity be interpreted through liminality, while recognising that students’ learning paths may differ?
Central outcomeKnowledge constructionConsidered alongside self-directed learning, problem solving, transfer, and learning transitions.

8.2 Philosophical grounding

A pragmatist way of looking at the study

Pragmatism guides the study because it brings practical consequences and contextual understanding into the interpretation of educational experience. Knowledge is considered in relation to the setting in which it is developed and the ways it helps us understand practice. This allows numerical evidence and students’ accounts to contribute to the same inquiry.

The approach has been discussed in relation to critical inquiry, interpretation, research methods, social and behavioural sciences, and domain-specific research (Peirce, 1905; Rorty, 1990; Murphy, 1990; Patton, 1990; Cherryholmes, 1992; Tashakkori & Teddlie, 2003; Morgan, 2014; Allemang et al., 2022).

01 · Ontology

What is real in this study?

Reality is understood through the course design, its delivery, and the experiences of people in the classroom. The study reports participants’ perspectives within this context.

02 · Epistemology

How was knowledge examined?

Knowledge was examined through methods that provide both quantitative evidence and qualitative meaning. Each source has strengths and limits.

03 · Axiology

How do values enter the inquiry?

Interpretation reflects both participant and researcher perspectives. Values and ethical considerations therefore remain part of the research process.

04 · Methodology

Which methods fit the questions?

Quantitative and qualitative methods are used together because the questions concern both observable learning evidence and students’ interpretations of experience.

8.3 Mixed methods design

Bringing numerical and narrative evidence together

The study uses a concurrent mixed-methods design in an engineering education context. Quantitative evidence describes patterns in understanding and performance. Qualitative evidence shows how students explain their learning, decisions, and transitions. Both strands were collected within the same study period and brought together during analysis and interpretation. Pragmatism provides the philosophical grounding, and liminality informs the interpretation of learning transitions.

TimingConcurrent collection

Quantitative and qualitative evidence were collected within the same study period.

Quantitative strandRatings and records

Survey ratings, concept-understanding responses, project evidence, and course scores.

Qualitative strandStudent accounts and context

Open survey responses interpreted alongside course and task materials.

IntegrationInterpretation

The strands were considered together in relation to the research questions.

8.4 Conceptual framework

Liminality as a lens on learning transitions

Liminality guides the interpretation of the transitions students may experience during the integration of PBL and CT. A liminal space is an in-between period marked by ambiguity, uncertainty, and change. In engineering education, students may be moving from theoretical knowledge toward the practical use of engineering ideas, while still developing confidence and competence (Morgan, 2012).

Students may encounter uncertainty, develop an emerging professional identity, build skills, and work to connect ideas. Guidance from educators, mentors, and industry professionals can help students navigate these transitions. Experiential learning, mentoring, and professional development can help bridge theory and practice (McCartney et al., 2009; Phillips, 2017).

The study uses liminality as an interpretive framework. Students’ reports of uncertainty and clarity are analysed as experiences; the design does not assume that each learner moves through a fixed or identical sequence.

8.5 Data collection

Evidence from the course and from students

The sources connect the research questions to the designed learning activities, course performance, and student experience. The final-study survey combines Likert items with open-ended prompts, including feedback on case-study reflection, project application, and learning transitions. Concept understanding, laboratory and project work, and theory and lab Continuous Internal Evaluation (CIE) reports provide performance evidence. The CIE reports were supplied as supplementary existing course records for this analysis. Theory scores comprise ISA 1 (out of 20), ISA 2 (out of 20), and Activity 1 (out of 10), with a total out of 50. Lab scores comprise three exercises out of 20, 20, and 40, with a total out of 80. In the completed analysis, a retrospective 50-mark portfolio-evidence rubric was applied to the latest submission for each roll number; 111 accessible portfolios were scored. Its six criteria were problem and real-world context (6), algorithm reasoning (10), implementation evidence (12), efficiency analysis (10), portfolio communication (6), and evidence of individual contribution (6). Marks were based on evidence visible in the portfolio, not on a student’s self-reported selection alone. This was an analytic review of documentation, not the original course grade, and is reported separately from CIE totals. Course-delivery notes, case-study materials, and lab exercise sheets are treated as instructional artefacts. The sixteen liminality case studies were used during the pilot and were also part of the official study. The official-study survey includes students’ feedback on case-study reflection and learning transitions; those responses are analysed separately from the pilot feedback.

Before the official-study survey, all participants were informed that the data collected would be used for research purposes. A consent statement on the standard survey form recorded their consent before they proceeded.

How to read the counts. The research unfolded from 2022 to 2025 through three stages, each with its own student batch: initial classroom exploration, the pilot study, and the official study. The stages are distinct and their records are not pooled. Within each stage, records come from that stage’s batch; counts vary by source because not every student completed every form or submitted every project, some students did not continue after getting a year back, and some records contain blank items, repeated submissions, or unavailable course data. Each analysis retains the count appropriate to its source; combined official-study results use matched IDs where required.

Qualitative accounts

Open survey responses, including students’ descriptions of project application, uncertainty, and transition. These accounts provide the student perspective on the learning tasks.

View the survey instrument

Likert items use a five-point scale from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). Question 5 is negatively worded and is reverse-coded for positively oriented summary scores. Open-ended prompts are shown separately and should be analysed as qualitative responses, not included in scale averages.

Section A · Conceptual understanding and knowledge construction

  1. The activities and explanations in this course helped me build a clearer understanding of each concept.
  2. I can now connect concepts across topics more effectively than before.
  3. The combination of problem cases, design intuitions, and classroom activities strengthened my conceptual foundation.
  4. I was able to construct my own understanding instead of relying only on direct instruction.
  5. My confidence in explaining course concepts in my own words has not at all improved.

Open response 6: Describe one moment or activity that significantly improved your understanding of a concept.

Section B · Self-directed learning

  1. The course motivated me to explore topics and examples beyond what was taught in class.
  2. I could plan, monitor, and evaluate my own progress throughout the course.
  3. The case studies encouraged me to reflect deeply on my learning process.
  4. I actively sought additional resources when I encountered difficulty.

Open response 11: Describe a situation where you took initiative in your learning during this course.

Section C · Problem-solving ability

  1. The case studies improved my ability to analyse and break down complex problems.
  2. I became more systematic in choosing data structures and algorithms.
  3. The lab exercises strengthened my confidence in solving programming problems independently.
  4. The design-based activities helped me learn how to approach unfamiliar problems.

Open response 16: Give an example of a problem you solved in this course and explain how your approach changed over time.

Section D · Transfer of knowledge

  1. I was able to apply concepts learned in class to the project work (city design and business cases).
  2. I can transfer what I learned in one topic to solve problems in another topic.
  3. The project helped me see how algorithms relate to real-world systems.
  4. I feel confident applying data structures and algorithmic thinking to new or unfamiliar contexts.

Open response 21: Describe how you used classroom learning in your project or other tasks.

Section E · Experience of liminality and learning transitions

  1. At some points in this course, I felt uncertain or stuck while learning.
  2. I eventually experienced clarity or breakthroughs after these phases.
  3. Reflective case studies helped me recognise transitions in my learning.
  4. I can identify moments where I moved from confusion to understanding.

Open response 26: Describe a transition where you moved from uncertainty to clarity. What helped you cross that threshold?

Section F · Course design and PBL–CT framework evaluation

  1. The PBL and CT elements in the course were clearly integrated.
  2. Activities required me to use decomposition, abstraction, pattern recognition, and algorithmic thinking.
  3. The structure of assignments and projects supported deep learning rather than surface-level learning.
  4. Overall, the course design helped me grow academically and personally.

Open responses 31–32: What aspect of the PBL–CT course design supported your learning the most? What aspect was least helpful or most challenging?

Section G · Overall outcome perception

  1. I feel I have grown as a problem solver.
  2. I feel more prepared to approach complex real-world computing problems.
  3. The course improved my ability to connect ideas, reflect, and learn independently.

Open response 36: Any additional comments or experiences you would like to share about your learning journey?

Section H · Additional knowledge construction insights

Open response 37: Describe how your understanding of a difficult concept evolved during the course. What steps, activities, or insights contributed to this shift?

Open response 38: Explain how you connected different concepts in the course to form a bigger picture of algorithms or problem-solving. What helped you make these connections?

Scoring alignment: The 28 Likert items are grouped into the seven survey domains. Question 5 is reverse-coded before calculating positively oriented summary scores. The duplicate Q34 column in the response export is excluded, retaining one Q34 item. Open-ended prompts remain qualitative evidence and are not included in scale averages.

8.7 Analysis and integration

Analysing the evidence in relation to the questions

The analysis brings together quantitative summaries, students’ qualitative accounts, the design of the course activities, and course performance. Survey findings are interpreted through the four learning dimensions of knowledge construction, self-directed learning, problem solving, and transfer of knowledge. Concept ratings, project evidence, and theory and lab scores provide complementary evidence about how these dimensions appeared in the course. Composite scores are interpreted cautiously alongside internal-consistency estimates.

01

Survey constructs

Report descriptive summaries. Estimate alpha and omega for the four core multi-item domains only: knowledge construction, self-directed learning, problem solving, and transfer. Interpret these estimates cautiously; report liminality items individually and do not treat the survey as a validated scale.

02

Performance and design evidence

Summarise laboratory, concept-understanding, and project records separately. Report the retrospective 50-mark portfolio rubric as a distinct analysis of accessible portfolio evidence, separate from CIE scores. Describe course and task materials as instructional artefacts. Treat case-study and project feedback as student perceptions, not performance scores.

03

Connecting evidence

Interpret survey ratings and open responses alongside concept understanding, project work, theory and lab scores, and the designed learning activities. Use the sources together to explain how self-directed learning, problem solving, and transfer contribute to knowledge construction.

04

Learning transitions

Describe liminality responses individually and connect students’ accounts of uncertainty, clarity, and reflection to the learning dimensions and activity designs discussed across the study.

05

Qualitative interpretation

Use a framework method with deductive and inductive coding to analyse open responses for autonomy, problem solving, transfer, uncertainty, clarity, and change. Keep each response attached to its prompt, compare recurring and divergent accounts, refine code families, and synthesize them into themes. Use task materials for context, not as student responses.

06

Triangulation

Examine where instructional materials, survey feedback, project portfolios, and performance evidence converge or differ. Agreement can strengthen an interpretation; differences show where intended design, student perceptions, and demonstrated performance do not align.

Interpretive approach. The study connects evidence at the level of learning dimensions and the course as a whole. Survey responses, concept ratings, project evidence, and CIE scores each contribute a different view of students’ learning. Their convergence supports an integrated explanation of knowledge construction without requiring every source to represent the same set of participants or making any single activity stand in for the whole framework. The three stages involve different batches; comparisons across evidence sources are made within the relevant stage, and the final KNOT framework is interpreted from the official-study evidence alongside the earlier stages that informed its development.

8.8 Bringing the design together

How the design brought the evidence together

The research questions, pragmatist grounding, PBL–CT course, liminality lens, instructional artefacts, student feedback, performance records, and analysis approach worked together. The course materials show how learning opportunities were designed; survey and open responses show how students reported experiencing and using them; performance measures describe course results. Their integration supports a contextual interpretation without treating design intentions, perceptions, and performance as interchangeable evidence.

This chapter documents the design used to examine the PBL–CT course. Chapter 7 presents KNOT Framework Version 0, which emerged from the pilot study before the final-study design. Chapters 9 and 10 report the final-study evidence, Chapter 11 discusses it in relation to the research questions, and Chapters 12–14 explain the emergence, philosophy, and final teacher-facing form of KNOT.
