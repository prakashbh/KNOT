Chapter 13 What this work has taught me

The KNOT's Philosophy

A belief about learning shaped by years of teaching, classroom experiments, and listening to students make sense of difficult ideas.

After years of teaching, one thing has stayed with me: a class becomes memorable when students have a reason to wonder, a way to work on the question, and room to change their minds.

13.1 · A term for the philosophy

Tied, Tried, Retied

Tied, Tried, Retied

Tied, Tried, Retied names the philosophy I draw from this study: learners tie ideas to a meaningful problem, try those connections through thinking and evidence, then retie them as they carry understanding into a new context. A knot is not a final answer. It is a connection that can be tested, loosened, strengthened, and made useful in what comes next.

“Learning is tied to a problem, tried through thinking, and retied when understanding travels into what comes next.”Tie it. Try it. Retie it.

13.2 · The philosophy

Learning is the work of making and remaking connections

For many years, I entered each class wondering what might make students curious that day. Each class offered another chance to notice what they understood, where an idea became difficult, and what might help them look again. This study grew from that question: how can a class invite students to think, not only receive what is already known?

The work has led me to believe that knowledge is not simply delivered. Students build it as they connect a new idea with what they know, use it to make sense of a problem, and check whether their explanation holds. A connection may be strong or incomplete. A new problem may show that an earlier understanding needs to be opened up and made again. That is part of learning.

A meaningful problem gives this work purpose and a reason to ask questions. Computational thinking offers ways to work through it: break it into parts, notice patterns, decide what matters, develop a procedure, and check whether it works. These practices help students give shape to their thinking so they can discuss, test, and improve it.

The case and lab sheets made this belief more concrete for me. The Hungry Loop Cafe case began with a service that slowed down when it repeatedly searched its records. The Invitation Problem began with visits students could imagine, then asked them to make decisions about routes, cost, time, assumptions, and a general algorithm. Lab 02 moved from familiar activities and shared expenses to programming and the comparison of possible solutions. I now see that a problem gives learning a beginning, but the deeper work is in how students represent it, explain a choice, inspect what happens, and connect the result to a principle.

Students also need room to be uncertain. Their accounts included effort, repeated practice, connecting topics, seeking help, and explaining ideas to peers. This does not mean struggle automatically produces learning. Students may need time, resources, conversation, feedback, and a safe opportunity to try again. A teacher's work is to notice when support can help a learner take the next step.

This is what I mean by Tied, Tried, Retied. Learners bring existing ideas into a new situation. A problem may strengthen a connection, reveal a gap, or bring separate topics together. The learner tests an explanation, revises it, and carries some understanding forward. Another context may ask for a new connection. Learning continues because what students know can become more useful, connected, and open to question.

Students’ project feedback helped me recognise this connection-making in the study itself. Many reported using class concepts in the project and seeing algorithms in real-world systems. In one open response, a student described applying graph theory to plan connected city roads. I read this alongside the submitted portfolios: they show what such application could look like in route planning, emergency services, resource allocation, and other city systems. The survey and portfolios do not prove that every student learned in the same way, but together they show why application and explanation belong in the philosophy of knowledge construction.

KNOT brings this belief into course design. Problem-Based Learning gives students a meaningful context; Computational Thinking helps them reason through it. Attention to liminality helps a teacher recognize unsettled understanding. Scaffolding, dialogue, practice, and feedback help students continue. Together, these elements can create conditions for knowledge construction, but they do not guarantee the same result for every student or form a fixed recipe.

I hope this work offers teachers permission to design with intention and remain responsive. Begin with a question worth exploring. Make students' thinking visible. Let them test and explain ideas. Offer support, and leave space for a new question. The final KNOT framework can be examined in other settings and adapted thoughtfully to local learners and subjects. Its value is in helping us ask better questions about how learning is designed and experienced.

My central belief is simple: understanding is not a finish line. It is a connection we make, examine, and carry into what comes next. When a class helps students do that for themselves, learning can continue beyond the lesson.

The work of teaching is not to tie every idea for a student. It is to create the conditions in which they can make, test, and remake the connections themselves.
