01 Learning changes with the world

Towards Foundations of Meaningful Learning

For most of history, education was informal and based on real-life experiences. In early societies, children learned by watching, imitating, and practicing tasks. Skills like farming, craftsmanship, and cultural traditions were passed down by elders through hands-on learning (Restuccia & Vandenbroucke, 2013). Though unstructured, this method was deeply connected to daily life. As civilizations developed, formal education systems emerged. Ancient cultures in Greece, India, and China introduced structured teaching. Philosophers such as Confucius and Socrates encouraged discussion, critical thinking, and moral growth (Archer, 2013). In India, the Gurukula system provided a mix of personalized instruction and holistic learning, where students lived with their teacher and learned through conversation, practice, and reflection (Selvamani, 2019).

The industrial revolution in the 18th and 19th centuries brought major changes. Factories needed skilled workers, and urban populations grew rapidly. Education became more standardized, focusing on lectures, memorization, and discipline. Schools followed an assembly-line approach, training students for structured, repetitive jobs. While this system met economic demands, it often limited creativity and critical thinking. Learning was treated as a process of receiving and memorizing information rather than exploring and applying knowledge (Kaestle & Vinovskis, M, 1980).

The 20th century saw new ideas in education. Thinkers like John Dewey, Maria Montessori, and Jean Piaget questioned traditional methods. They promoted active learning, student involvement, and the importance of real-world experiences. Dewey, in particular, believed education should prepare students for democratic society by encouraging problem-solving, creativity, and adaptability instead of rigid conformity (Breen, 2010). Technology also changed the way people learned. Radio, television, and later, the internet made knowledge more accessible, reducing the role of schools as the only source of education. Learning was no longer limited to classrooms. Globalization and technological advancements further reshaped education. With the rise of computers, automation, and artificial intelligence, routine tasks became less important, and innovation, adaptability, and problem-solving became essential. The World Economic Forum identified analytical thinking, complex problem-solving, critical thinking, creativity, and collaboration among skills that employers considered important (World Economic Forum, 2018). Employers look for individuals who can handle uncertainty, combine different viewpoints, and develop solutions to complex problems, skills that go beyond traditional schooling (Hafeez, 2021).

We can observe that education has constantly evolved over time, influenced by the needs, challenges, and goals of different periods. In the past, people learned through apprenticeships, where skills were passed down directly. Later, mass education systems became common, especially in the 20th century. These changes reflected the values of each society. As the world becomes more complex and interconnected, traditional teaching methods struggle to prepare students for modern challenges. Looking at how education has developed helps in understanding what improvements are needed to equip learners for the future (Srivastava, 2023).

Many education systems still rely on out-dated methods. Lecture-based teaching is common, focusing more on delivering content than applying it. Exams often test memorization instead of understanding, making it harder for students to use what they learn in real-life situations. Subjects are taught separately, ignoring how different fields connect in solving modern problems. Challenges like climate change, public health issues, and ethical concerns in technology require knowledge from multiple areas. Solving these problems takes more than technical skills. It requires teamwork, an understanding of social impacts, and the ability to think critically about long-term effects. Traditional education often encourages students to stay within strict academic boundaries, valuing compliance over creativity (Tularam, 2018).

Many students feel disconnected from their studies. Research indicates that student engagement tends to decline as they progress through their education. Studies have found that between 40% and 60% of students exhibit signs of disengagement, such as lack of involvement, apathy, minimal effort, and inattentiveness, during their secondary school years (Wang & Fredricks, 2014). Many struggle to see how their lessons relate to life after graduation. This disconnect highlights the need for approaches that make learning more relevant. As we see and witness today, knowledge quickly becomes out-dated. The fast pace of change means that learning facts is not enough. Students must develop the ability to learn, unlearn, and adapt to new situations. Problem-solving, adaptability, and innovation are essential skills. Collaboration is also more important than ever. Many engineering challenges, for example, involve teams from different fields and countries. Education must prepare students to work with diverse perspectives, communicate clearly, and find common ground. Technology is changing how people learn. AI, virtual reality, and online platforms create new ways to personalize learning and make it more engaging. However, technology alone is not the solution. Its impact depends on how well it is used within strong teaching methods that encourage meaningful problem-solving.

As expectations for education change, the weaknesses of traditional methods become clearer. A shift is needed from simply delivering knowledge to encouraging active learning, collaboration, and real-world problem-solving. Looking at past and present ideas about education shows a clear trend that students need more than memorization and problem-solving (Tahiru, 2021). This shift opens the door for new approaches. When students engage with real-world challenges, they bridge the gap between theory and practice. These methods help prepare them for a world where adaptability and creativity are essential. Rethinking education in this way can ensure it stays relevant in a hastily changing world.

At the heart of this rethinking lies the concept of knowledge construction (Resnick et al., 1991). Rather than viewing learning as the passive absorption of information, knowledge construction emphasizes how learners actively build, refine, and connect ideas through meaningful experiences (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). Rooted in constructivist theories, KC emphasizes that learning is not a passive transfer of facts but an active, interpretive, and often social process (Piaget, 1972; Vygotsky, 1978). Unlike knowledge acquisition, which suggests the mere transfer or storage of facts, knowledge construction refers to the process by which individuals interpret, organize, and connect information to create meaningful understanding. It highlights that understanding is not transferred from teacher to student but co-created through interaction, inquiry, and reflection (Jonassen, 1999). This perspective is especially important today, as students are expected to deal with complex, ill-structured problems that require integrating diverse forms of knowledge (Bruner, 1996).

Knowledge construction also connects deeply with metacognition, where learners become aware of and regulate their own thinking, leading to more durable and transferable understanding (King, 2002). Scholars argue that it nurtures academic success and the capacity to adapt and innovate in professional and social contexts (Palincsar, 1998). Placing knowledge construction at the center of educational practice promises that learning extends beyond classrooms, equipping students with the skills to apply, adapt, and create knowledge in new situations (Hmelo-Silver, 2004).

This broader shift in education points toward a deeper understanding of how learners actually make sense of what they encounter. Moving beyond the delivery of information, contemporary thought emphasizes the ways in which learners interpret, connect, and transform knowledge through their own experiences and interactions. Exploring this dimension provides valuable insights into why certain approaches encourage lasting engagement and transferable skills, and it sets the stage for examining the underlying processes that make learning meaningful.

02 Three connected strands

Elements of Knowledge Construction

Constructing knowledge is not a linear act but a dynamic process shaped by the interplay of ideas, practices, and learner capacities. At its core, knowledge construction can be seen as the convergence of three interrelated dimensions. Learning theories provide the conceptual grounding, helping us understand how individuals absorb, interpret, and internalize knowledge. Pedagogical approaches offer the practical means of translating these theoretical insights into learning experiences, shaping the ways knowledge is actively built rather than passively received. Finally, learner competencies determine the extent to which individuals can engage with, apply, and extend what they learn across contexts. When viewed together, these dimensions reveal a holistic picture in which theory, practice, and capacity mutually reinforce one another, laying the foundations for meaningful and lasting learning. The components can be seen in Figure 1 below.

At the core of meaningful learning lies knowledge construction, a process through which learners actively build, connect, and reorganize understanding rather than passively absorb information. To operationalize this central process, three interrelated pathways converge towards facilitating knowledge construction.

01

Learning theories

Learning Theories serve as the conceptual foundation for understanding why and how learners construct knowledge. Rooted in constructivism, these theories posit that knowledge is not transmitted but co-constructed through cognitive and social processes such as assimilation, accommodation, and scaffolding (Piaget, 1972; Vygotsky, 1978). This makes learning both interpretive and reflective, as learners integrate new information with prior understanding.

02

Pedagogical approaches

Pedagogical Approaches provide the strategies that schools and educators can employ to support this constructive process. We need pedagogies that prompt learners to confront real-world problems, promoting self-directed inquiry and collaborative meaning-making, the trademarks of active knowledge construction (Yew et al., 2011). We also need cognitive processes aligned with how learners build and apply knowledge in complex domains.

03

Learner competencies

Learner Competencies reflect on the mastery of content and also the capacity to adapt, integrate, and apply understanding in novel contexts. They represent the learner’s ability to move beyond surface-level recall and engage in deeper problem-solving and transfer of knowledge. In this sense, competencies serve as the connection that connects what is learned with how it can be meaningfully used across diverse and evolving situations.

In brief, the three strands help explain:

  • Why and how knowledge is constructed.
  • How educators can design experiences that support construction.
  • What learners need to sustain that process in practice.

Intertwining these three strands around knowledge construction, grounding on why it has to be valued, provided how it will be enabled, and identifying what competencies result from it, a cohesive and theoretically anchored framework can be articulated for meaningful learning.

03 From broad frameworks to practice

Global Competency Frameworks for Knowledge Construction

Global frameworks such as the Partnership for 21st Century Skills (P21), the Accreditation Board for Engineering and Technology (ABET), and the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) have progressively highlighted the multidimensional nature of knowledge construction. These frameworks converge on the idea that the development of knowledge cannot be reduced to the acquisition of isolated facts but rather requires the cultivation of transferable skills, conceptual depth, and adaptive capacities. They highlight that preparing learners for contemporary challenges involves mastering disciplinary content and developing the cognitive, social, and metacognitive abilities necessary to work on complex problems, collaborate across domains, and extend knowledge to novel contexts. When situated in the context of education, these perspectives emphasize that knowledge construction emerges from the intersection of three critical dimensions: learning theories, pedagogical approaches, and learner competencies.

Learning theories

Learning theories provide the foundational lenses through which we understand how individuals absorb, process, and internalize knowledge. Constructivist perspectives, for instance, argue that knowledge is not passively received but actively built through engagement, reflection, and meaning-making (Piaget, 1970; Vygotsky, 1978). This notion has been extended by social constructivist theorists who emphasize the role of collaboration, dialogue, and cultural context in shaping understanding. Cognitive learning theories, including information-processing models, highlight how learners encode, store, and retrieve knowledge, while behaviorist approaches emphasize the importance of reinforcement and feedback in shaping performance (Skinner, 1953). More contemporary frameworks such as connectivism emphasize learning as the ability to form and navigate networks of information, skills, and social connections in the digital age (Siemens, 2005). Taken together, these theories disclose that no single perspective is sufficient; instead, a synthesis of theoretical insights provides a richer understanding of how knowledge is developed and sustained. They highlight that knowledge construction involves iterative cycles of internalization, externalization, collaboration, and application.

Pedagogical approaches

Pedagogical approaches represent the translation of these theories into practice, offering educators concrete strategies to structure meaningful learning experiences. Project-Based Learning allows learners to apply knowledge over extended tasks culminating in tangible outputs, thereby emphasizing creativity, collaboration, and iterative refinement (Blumenfeld et al., 1991). Inquiry-Based Learning positions learners as active investigators, guided by curiosity and evidence, promoting critical thinking and self-directed exploration (Pedaste et al., 2015). Design-Based Learning further integrates the iterative processes of prototyping and testing, especially valuable in engineering and technology education (Kolodner et al., 2003). Similarly, Case-Based Learning situates learners in real-world decision-making scenarios that require contextual reasoning and application of theory to practice (Kim et al., 2006). Problem-Based Learning (PBL) is one such approach, wherein learners engage with authentic, ill-structured problems that require investigation, reasoning, and synthesis, thereby fostering deep understanding and transferable skills (Barrows, 1986). Computational Thinking (CT) has gained prominence for cultivating problem-solving skills through abstraction, decomposition, algorithmic reasoning, and pattern recognition (Wing, 2006). Yet, these are only part of a broader landscape of effective pedagogies. These approaches, while varied, are unified by a common thread: they position learners as active agents in their learning journey, emphasize higher-order thinking, and seek to bridge theory with real-world application. Their diversity reflects the multiple pathways through which the insights of learning theories can be actualized in educational practice.

Learner competencies

Learner competencies constitute the third dimension, shaping the extent to which individuals can engage with and extend knowledge. These competencies extend far beyond content mastery, encompassing cognitive, interpersonal, and intrapersonal domains. The P21 framework identifies critical competencies such as creativity and innovation, communication, collaboration, information literacy, and life and career skills (Partnership for 21st Century Skills, 2009). Similarly, ABET outcomes emphasize the ability to apply knowledge of mathematics, science, and engineering, design and conduct experiments, function on multidisciplinary teams, engage in lifelong learning, and understand professional and ethical responsibilities (ABET, 2022). IEEE standards reinforce comparable expectations for technical proficiency, global awareness, and leadership capacities in engineering education (IEEE, 2021). Competencies can thus be grouped into clusters such as:

01

Cognitive

Cognitive competencies: critical thinking, problem-solving, abstraction, systems thinking, analytical reasoning, decision-making.

02

Interpersonal

Interpersonal competencies: teamwork, communication (oral and written), leadership, negotiation, cultural sensitivity.

03

Intrapersonal

Intrapersonal competencies: self-directed learning, adaptability, resilience, ethical reasoning, metacognition, emotional regulation.

04

Technological

Technological competencies: digital literacy, computational reasoning, data analysis, responsible use of AI tools.

When viewed together, these dimensions highlight that learners require an integrated skill set that enables the disciplinary mastery, the adaptability and foresight to transfer knowledge across contexts and disciplines. Competencies provide the link between theoretical grounding and pedagogical enactment, determining how learners can actually mobilize knowledge for meaningful purposes.

Learner competencies reflect on mastery of content and the capacity to adapt, integrate, and apply understanding in novel contexts. They mark the point where theoretical insights and pedagogical designs converge in practice, determining the depth and transferability of learning outcomes. These competencies encompass the ability to critically evaluate solutions, the creativity to envision new solutions, the resilience to navigate ambiguity, and the judgment to act appropriately in a globalized, interconnected world. As emphasized by global skill frameworks, such competencies are not static end-points but dynamic capacities that evolve as learners encounter new challenges and environments. Thus, the emphasis on competencies ensures that knowledge construction is not limited to academic performance but extends to lifelong learning, civic participation, and professional growth.

Taken holistically, the interaction of learning theories, pedagogical approaches, and learner competencies provides a comprehensive model of knowledge construction. Learning theories supply the conceptual foundations for how individuals learn, pedagogical approaches operationalize these insights into meaningful learning environments, and competencies represent the learner’s capacity to engage with, extend, and apply this learning. By embedding these dimensions within globally recognized frameworks such as P21, ABET, and IEEE, education can align with both disciplinary rigor and the evolving demands of society.

04 The focus of this study

Core Competencies for Advancing Knowledge Construction

Building on the theoretical foundations, elements of knowledge construction, and global competency frameworks, it becomes essential to narrow the focus towards a set of core learner competencies that are most relevant for advancing meaningful learning in computer science education. While frameworks such as the Partnership for 21st Century Skills, ABET, and IEEE identify a wide array of competencies, from collaboration and communication to lifelong learning, this work emphasizes three specific competencies: problem solving, self-directed learning, and transfer of knowledge.

01 / SOLVE

Problem solving

Use abstraction, decomposition, pattern recognition, and algorithmic reasoning to work through complex and unfamiliar challenges.

02 / DIRECT

Self-directed learning

Set a direction, notice what needs to be learned, and adapt as technologies and professional needs change.

03 / CARRY

Transfer of knowledge

Apply concepts learned in one setting to a new situation, connecting classroom understanding with real-world use.

Problem solving is central to engineering and computer science, as it requires learners to apply abstraction, decomposition, pattern recognition and algorithmic reasoning to tackle complex and novel challenges. Self-directed learning ensures that students can adapt to rapidly evolving technological landscapes, cultivating autonomy and reflective practices essential for professional growth. Transfer of knowledge, meanwhile, represents the higher-order ability to apply concepts learned in one context to unfamiliar scenarios, thereby bridging classroom understanding with real-world applications.

These three competencies were chosen because they integrate the strengths of pedagogical approaches such as Problem-Based Learning and Computational Thinking while extending their scope. All of these together establish a robust foundation for learners to construct knowledge meaningfully and adaptively.

Recent research and educational practices increasingly emphasize these competencies, recognizing their role in preparing learners for complex, uncertain, and interconnected futures. Studies highlight how pedagogical approaches like Problem-Based Learning, Computational Thinking, and interdisciplinary frameworks nurture these skills, while global benchmarks such as ABET and IEEE continue to refine them into measurable outcomes. Building on this momentum, Chapter 2, Background Study, surveys the literature surrounding these and related areas, tracing how scholarship and practice converge to shape meaningful learning in computer science education.

Short narrative inserts

Learning as lived experience

Long before theories were written, learning happened in the quiet rhythm of daily life. Children watched adults tend to crops, repair tools, or solve disputes in the village courtyard. Learning happened through the stories shared at dusk and through the repeated attempts to master skills with guidance and patience. These early forms reveal something timeless: learning becomes meaningful when it is woven into lived experience.

From industrial to modern learning

When factories replaced fields, classrooms began to resemble the assembly lines they served. Rows of benches, identical textbooks, timed bells, and fixed syllabi shaped the culture of learning. The goal was efficiency. Yet beneath this structure lay a silence about curiosity, exploration, or self-expression. The world has changed since then, but many classrooms still carry the echoes of that era.

The learner in a changing world

Today’s learner stands in a world where knowledge multiplies every hour. Tools evolve, industries transform, and new ethical questions surface with every technological shift. In such a world, the most valuable learner is not the one who remembers the most but the one who can adapt, question, rebuild, and transfer what they know.

A moment of knowledge construction

When a learner encounters a new idea, something subtle begins inside the mind. They test the idea against what they already know. They reshape their understanding. They question their assumptions. They speak to peers. They revise their thinking. This silent, internal movement is the true heart of knowledge construction.

A problem-based learning scene

Imagine a group of students facing a real-world challenge: designing an emergency evacuation plan for a crowded urban zone. They study maps, write algorithms to model movement, consider human behavior in panic, and debate trade-offs in their approach. There is no single correct answer. Through this struggle, learning becomes alive.
